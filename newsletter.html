No. 012 — THURSDAY, AUGUST 13, 2026 — 19 ITEMS — 12-SOURCE ROSTER
CFO Brief — three takeaways, written to be read aloud
  1. Severity — a storm outbreak crossed five Midwestern states this week, and the broker modelling it thinks it may land in the industry's top ten. Guy Carpenter, reported today by Artemis, says the 9–11 August severe convective storm outbreak across Wisconsin, Illinois, Iowa, Indiana and Ohio could breach the top-ten severe-convective-storm loss list, which currently requires roughly $5.1bn to enter. Sugar Grove, Illinois recorded a 97 mph gust and parts of the southern Chicago metro exceeded 90 mph, with hail to 3.5 inches. That is a broker's early estimate rather than a settled figure, and an industry-wide number rather than a statement about any one company. The action it prompts is time-sensitive and conditional on your own footprint: identify which sites, critical supplier locations and in-transit goods sat inside that area, and where any did, get the damage walked, photographed and documented now, with notice given to carriers as required. Wind and hail damage to roofs and cladding is routinely missed in the first week because it is not visible from the ground, and late-discovered damage is the version that gets argued about at claim time.
  2. Market — the softening is no longer uniform, and the first line has flattened out. Primary directors' and officers' pricing rose 0.6% in the second quarter against the same quarter last year, ending sixteen consecutive quarters of decline, on Aon data reported by Risk & Insurance. The detail matters more than the headline: 47% of renewals were flat, 28% rose and 25% fell, and the median change was zero — a market that has stopped falling and tipped very slightly positive on average, not one turning hard. Against that, property remains soft enough that broker Amwins warned today that insurance-to-value discipline built during the hard market is already eroding. The planning line is that the across-the-board reduction is ending line by line rather than all at once, which argues for treating part of the remaining property saving as something to reinvest in valuation accuracy and programme structure rather than banking all of it. This is one broker's index of one line, and says nothing directly about where any individual property or casualty renewal lands.
  3. Exposure — the cyber losses actually being recorded this year are not the ones the market is talking about. Cyber insurer Resilience, in claims data reported by Risk & Insurance, found social engineering involved in 85.3% of incurred cyber losses in the first half of 2026, up from 17.7% two years earlier, with no losses attributed to AI-specific attack vectors. Extortion accounted for 73% of incurred losses from only 5.8% of claims. Those two figures overlap rather than sum — social engineering is the way in, extortion is what the loss becomes — and "incurred" includes reserves, so it is not the same as cash paid. It is one carrier's book, and an absence of AI-coded losses may reflect how claims are categorised as much as what happened. Discounted for all of that, it still points somewhere specific: losses are running through people being deceived into authorising payments and access, so core payment-verification and privileged-access controls and finance-team training are where the evidence currently supports spending, alongside rather than instead of newer AI-threat tooling.

01

August 9–11 US Midwest storm outbreak may rank as a top-10 industry loss

Artemis, citing reinsurance broker Guy Carpenter · August 13 · Broker loss estimate

Guy Carpenter said insured losses from the 9–11 August severe convective storm outbreak are likely to be measured in billions of dollars, with the possibility of entering the top-ten severe convective storm loss list, which requires around $5.1bn. Wisconsin, Illinois, Iowa, Indiana and Ohio were affected by 60–80 mph gusts, with a 97 mph reading at Sugar Grove, Illinois and over 90 mph in the southern Chicago metro; hail reached 3.5 inches and rainfall ran 2–3 inches per hour.

Why it matters — the only item today with a same-week deadline attached. An industry loss at this scale is unlikely on its own to reverse a soft property market, though it can still affect regional appetite, catastrophe aggregates and wind and hail deductible terms at renewal, so the value is mostly in claims handling rather than market commentary. Two things are worth doing this week for any location inside that area. Physically inspect roofs, rooftop equipment and cladding, because hail and wind damage invisible from the ground routinely resurfaces months later as a water loss the carrier then disputes — and check the notice requirement and any separate wind or hail deductible before assuming a small loss is not worth reporting. Then identify single-source suppliers inside those five states, noting the coverage mechanics: contingent business interruption generally responds only to insured physical damage at a dependent property, and often only at scheduled locations, so a supplier merely being in an affected state is a supply-chain question before it is an insurance one.

02

Primary D&O pricing firms up at midyear, ending a 16-quarter streak of rate declines

Risk & Insurance, citing data from broker Aon's Financial Services Group · August 10 · Broker-published data

Aon's Q2 2026 pricing index recorded a 0.6% year-on-year increase in primary D&O policy pricing at constant limit and deductible — the first rise after sixteen straight quarterly declines. The median and modal change were both zero: 47% of policies renewed flat, 28% increased (average +7.4%) and 25% decreased (average −5.7%). Average price per million of coverage still fell 1.9%, carrier retention was 96%, and 49 federal securities class actions were filed in the quarter, five more than a year earlier.

Why it matters — the most consequential market datapoint this week, and the distribution is more informative than the average. A small positive average against a zero median describes a market that has flattened, with the largest single group of buyers — a plurality at 47%, not a majority — renewing flat. Average price per million is still falling, which at index level means limit has been getting cheaper per unit; whether that holds for any individual tower depends on attachment, loss record and carrier mix, so it is a reason to test an increase with the broker rather than assume one is available. And the flattening is line-specific: this is primary D&O in one broker's book, and nothing here says property or casualty has turned, so a broker presenting a single blended market story across the programme should be asked to break it out by line.

03

AI data centre boom is 'maxing out' P&C insurers, AIG chief executive says

Insurance Journal · August 13 · Insurer executive commentary

AIG chief executive Eric Andersen told Bloomberg TV that AI data centre development is straining industry capacity, spanning construction, cyber, property and liability: "It is absolutely maxing out the P&C insurance industry in terms of the limits that are required." He framed it as a growth opportunity as well as a constraint. This is an insurer chief executive describing his own market, so it is commentary rather than measured data, and he has a commercial interest in the capacity narrative.

Why it matters — a signal to watch rather than a finding to act on. Capacity is not a single pool: construction, cyber and specialised property limits are underwritten by different teams with different appetites and are not freely interchangeable with capacity for an established industrial manufacturer. What a genuine squeeze in adjacent classes would do, if it materialises, is show up in the shape of the offer as much as the price — slower quoting, smaller line sizes per carrier and more layers to build the same tower. Since it costs nothing to guard against, the sensible response is timing: start property renewal marketing earlier than usual this year, so that if appetite is slower to assemble there is room to absorb it.

04

Soft property market sets up an insurance-to-value reckoning, Amwins warns

Insurance Business America, citing wholesale broker Amwins · August 13 · Broker commentary · Real estate practice figures

Amwins' national real estate property practice leader warned that competitive pricing is eroding the underwriting discipline built during the hard market: "In the soft market, things start to deteriorate that we had built up in the hard market, like insurance to value." He cautioned that undervalued properties face double corrections when capacity tightens. A colleague on the casualty side said casualty is becoming more balanced, with annual increases moderating from 10–15% to 3–5% and flat renewals no longer unusual.

Why it matters — broker commentary with an obvious commercial angle, since brokers benefit from clients revisiting valuations, but the mechanism is real and it echoes the Marsh underinsurance warning carried yesterday from an independent direction. Declared values drift below replacement cost whenever construction and equipment cost inflation continues and no underwriter is pushing back. Whether that bites at claim time depends on the policy: an average or coinsurance adjustment applies only where the policy actually contains a coinsurance or margin clause and it has not been overridden by an agreed-value or blanket arrangement, which is worth confirming rather than assuming in either direction. Treat the casualty figures with care — they come from a real estate practice, not a manufacturing book, so they signal that casualty pressure is easing rather than providing a benchmark for a manufacturer's renewal budget.

05

UnitedHealth investor suit alleges security and governance lapses

Insurance Journal · August 13 · D&O litigation · Allegations, untested in court

Shareholders led by a US state public employee retirement system and a Swedish asset manager have sued twelve current and former UnitedHealth directors and officers in Minnesota federal court, alleging the board ignored governance and oversight risks for years. The complaint alleges lax cybersecurity following the $7.8bn Change Healthcare acquisition contributed to the 2024 breach affecting 190 million people, that an internal Medicare billing audit programme was shut down, and that a federal policy change costing roughly $11bn over three years was known from 2023 but not disclosed until 2025. These allegations have not been tested, and the reporting does not establish whether the action is framed as a derivative or a securities claim — which matters, because it determines how a D&O tower responds.

Why it matters — read alongside the D&O pricing item as an illustration of the claim environment underwriters are pricing for, not as the cause of the pricing change; no single filing explains an index. The structure of the claim is what travels: an oversight case, alleging directors failed to monitor known risks rather than that they acted wrongly themselves, with its cybersecurity strand grounded in an acquisition. For any acquisitive manufacturer, cyber and compliance diligence on a target — and what the board is subsequently told about the gaps found — is a documented D&O exposure and not only an IT integration question. The defensible position is a board record showing the risk was identified, quantified and tracked to closure, and separately, confirmation that Side A cover would still protect individual directors if the company were unable to indemnify them.

06

Homeowners insurance market reaches a 'fragmented phase', says S&P Global Market Intelligence

Insurance Journal, citing S&P Global Market Intelligence · August 13 · Personal lines rate data

National effective approved homeowners rate change has fallen from 13.6% in 2024 to 6.3% in 2025 and 1.8% through July 2026. State-level moderation is sharp — Minnesota from 17.8% to 1.6%, Colorado from 16.6% to 0.8%. S&P GMI describes carriers as having largely completed a national adequacy reset and now pricing state by state, using deductibles, risk tiering and underwriting selection rather than broad rate.

Why it matters — personal lines, so there is no direct commercial read-across, and it is carried for one structural reason. It shows what the end of a correction looks like from the inside: not a reversal, but a shift from blunt rate action to selective terms. Commercial property is a different market on a different clock — rate-regulated personal lines and negotiated commercial placements do not move in step, so no timetable should be inferred — but the tools are the same, and deductible structure, sub-limits and tiering by location quality are where a softening commercial market tends to reprice risk quietly once headline rate stops moving. The practical version is to read the renewal terms rather than only the premium line, because that is where any change is likely to appear first.

Insurtech funding reached $2.44bn in Q2 2026, the highest since Q2 2022, with AI-focused companies taking 99.1% of the capital — $2.42bn across 95 of 107 deals. Sites presented with documented protection, current valuations and clean loss narratives separate further from those presented as a schedule of addresses — the same conclusion the valuation item reaches by another route. The drop in early-stage funding is a single quarter and should not be read as a lasting change in market structure.

08

Jury finds seat belt maker liable in $40.5m child injury case

Missouri Lawyers Media · Reported July 22 · Verdict returned July 2 · Older but critical · Product liability

A Jackson County, Missouri jury returned $40.5m over injuries to a six-year-old passenger in a 2018 rear-end collision on Interstate 70 — $8m compensatory against the manufacturer and the driver, and $32.5m punitive against the intoxicated driver alone. Fault was apportioned 51% to the driver and 49% to TRW/ZF Passive Safety Systems, whose seat belt restraint the jury found failed to protect the child's upper torso despite correct booster seat use. The manufacturer argued its design complied with federal safety standards.

Why it matters — carried from July because today's verdict sweep produced nothing new, and because this is the most directly relevant recent result to a component manufacturer, closer to the industrial world than the nursing-home and pharmaceutical verdicts that dominate the nuclear verdict literature. Three points. The $40.5m headline is not the manufacturer's exposure: the punitive award landed on the driver alone, so the component maker's share is roughly 49% of the $8m compensatory award, on the order of $3.9m before post-trial motions, appeal, or the operation of state rules on comparative fault and joint and several liability. Second, compliance with federal safety standards was argued and did not carry the day at trial; in most jurisdictions regulatory compliance is evidence of due care rather than a complete defence, though preemption arguments can survive to appeal. Third, and the reason to keep it in front of a CFO: a component supplier several steps removed from the injured party still carried nearly half the fault.

09

Insurance considerations for companies facing social media harm litigation

Risk Management Magazine (RIMS) · August 11 · Coverage analysis by policyholder-side counsel

The authors set out which policies may respond to social media harm claims: technology errors and omissions for negligent design, commercial general liability for bodily injury or public nuisance theories, media liability for content vendors, and D&O for wrongful acts by leadership. On occurrence-based policies they walk through the four trigger approaches courts apply — exposure, manifestation, injury-in-fact and continuous — noting that harm alleged to develop over years can implicate multiple policy years. They expect insurers to argue intentional conduct exclusions and to dispute whether psychological harm is bodily injury. The authors are policyholder-side coverage lawyers, so the analysis is written from the insured's side of the argument.

Why it matters — the subject matter is not industrial, but the trigger analysis is why it is worth reading. Any claim alleging harm that accumulates over years — occupational disease, chemical or noise exposure, a component degrading in service — raises the same question of which policy years are on risk. One correction to the instinctive conclusion, because it is the kind of thing that gets promised to a CFO and then not delivered: multiple triggered years do not automatically mean multiple limits. Non-cumulation and anti-stacking provisions, allocation rules that vary by jurisdiction, self-insured retentions in each year, exhaustion, and insurers that no longer exist all cut against a simple stack. Note too that the four occurrence triggers are a general-liability analysis and do not transfer to claims-made D&O, E&O or media wordings, which respond by reference to when the claim is made. The unglamorous practical implication stands regardless: complete historical policy records — forms, endorsements, binders, notices and predecessor-entity records — are worth a great deal when a long-tail claim arrives, and reconstructing them at that point is too late.

10

California cumulative trauma claims nearly double since 2018, with Southern California attorneys driving the shift

Risk & Insurance, citing the California Workers' Compensation Institute · August 10 · Research institute data

CWCI found cumulative trauma claims rose from 9.1% of all California workers' compensation claims in 2018 to 16.5% in 2025, driven mainly by more workers filing a first CT claim rather than repeat filings. Soft tissue CT volume rose 298% and mental disorder claims 253% over the period. Southern California law firms handled 23% of CT cases involving Northern California employers in 2019, rising to 79% by 2025.

Why it matters — a single-state workers' compensation trend, and it sits in this section because the striking figure is about lawyers rather than injuries. Filings concentrating in specialist firms operating far from the employer's location is consistent with referral-driven claims activity rather than with changing plant conditions, though the data as presented does not exclude other explanations: venue selection, representation rates and shifts in the employer mix could all contribute. Whether the pattern migrates to other states is genuinely unknown, since California's cumulative trauma rules are unusually permissive and that is part of why the concentration is visible there. The defence does not depend on resolving any of that, because it is entirely operational: contemporaneous documentation of job demands, ergonomic assessments and exit examinations. A cumulative trauma claim filed after employment ends is defended with the records that existed at the time, or it is not defended at all.

Parametric & Alternative Risk11–14
11

Liberty Mutual Re makes a sensor-triggered parametric earthquake payout in Peru

Artemis · August 13 · Parametric

A magnitude 5.8 earthquake at Ica, Peru on 19 May 2026 triggered a payout under Liberty Mutual Reinsurance's ShakeNet Parametric policy, placed by a Latin American broker and triggered by Safehub's building-mounted sensor network rather than by a regional index. Funds were released 21 days after the event. The policyholder bought the cover in October 2025 to sit alongside traditional insurance. The payout amount was not disclosed.

Why it matters — the most practically instructive parametric item to appear in this brief's run, because it goes at the objection that normally kills parametric proposals. Index triggers carry basis risk — the index fires when the building is undamaged, or fails to fire when it is wrecked — and a sensor mounted on the actual structure narrows the geographic part of that gap. It does not remove basis risk: measured shaking is not damage, and sensor placement, trigger thresholds, the payout curve and the building's own vulnerability all still sit between the reading and the loss. For a manufacturer with concentrated production in a seismic or windstorm region that still makes parametric cover worth pricing as a complement to traditional property insurance, valuable mainly for speed of cash. Two things to be precise about with a CFO: the payout is set by the trigger schedule rather than by the loss, so it will not track the property deductible or the actual damage, and 21 days is fast for insurance but is not the same-week liquidity some parametric marketing implies.

Rise of alternative capital will create greater space for financial investors, says Guy Carpenter's Rousseau

Artemis · August 13 · Broker executive commentary · Forward-looking opinion

Guy Carpenter's chief executive of global capital and advisory for Europe and IMEA said financial investors currently supply over $120bn of roughly $700bn in total reinsurance capital, a share he characterised as above 20% and expects to head toward a third over the next five years. Note that the two figures as reported imply about 17%, so the "20% plus" characterisation is his and does not follow arithmetically from the numbers given. He described the friction as two industries speaking different languages, and expects reinsurers to remain the risk-selection leaders with investors becoming educated followers. This is a five-year forecast, not measured data.

Why it matters — the structural reason property catastrophe pricing has stayed soft, stated as a trend rather than a moment. Capital arriving from investment markets rather than insurance balance sheets is less attached to relationships and can reprice faster, and a rising share of it means more capacity chasing catastrophe risk. Two qualifications keep this honest. That capital sits mostly in catastrophe reinsurance and collateralised structures, so the effect on a manufacturer's primary property programme is indirect, arriving through what reinsurance costs its carriers. And it is not frictionlessly mobile — collateral can be trapped after a loss event while claims develop. The direction it argues for is still worth naming: where a carrier will write multi-year structure on good terms, taking it is worth more than treating each soft renewal as if it were permanent, subject to reading the cancellation and reinstatement provisions before calling anything locked in.

14

CatIQ finalises Hurricane Debby flood loss estimate for Ontario and Quebec at C$2.80bn

Artemis, citing Canadian catastrophe data aggregator CatIQ · August 13 · Finalised loss estimate

CatIQ finalised insured losses from the Hurricane Debby remnant flooding in Ontario and Quebec at C$2.80bn, covering commercial and residential property and motor claims.

Why it matters — relevant to any operation or supplier base in central Canada, and a reminder that the damaging phase of a tropical system is frequently inland freshwater flooding hundreds of miles from landfall, well after the system has stopped being called a hurricane. That distinction is a coverage question rather than a meteorological one. Flood is treated very differently across property programmes — excluded in some, endorsed back with its own sub-limit and deductible in others, and occasionally written to full limit — and named-storm definitions vary on whether they follow the originating system once it becomes a remnant or turn on an agency designation at the time and place of the damage. The question worth putting to a broker is therefore specific to the wording: which peril definition and which deductible would apply to remnant flooding from a named storm at an inland site, and is the flood sub-limit sized against the current values at the sites actually exposed.

Manufacturing & Workplace15–17
15

Insurance programmes may miss Lithium Triangle losses, WTW warns

Insurance Business America, citing broker WTW's Willis Research Network · August 13 · Broker research

WTW research with an academic economist examined the Lithium Triangle — Chile, Argentina and Bolivia, holding 40–45% of identified global lithium resources — and found the dominant loss drivers are non-damage: operating restrictions, government-imposed access controls, labour action, fiscal intervention, foreign-exchange constraints, delayed settlement and non-payment. Three scenarios were tested: a high-altitude earthquake triggering water contamination concerns and operating restrictions, an export levy during a balance-of-payments crisis prompting capital controls, and an automation-driven port strike at Antofagasta. WTW noted exposure extends through supply chains and financing structures.

Why it matters — the most transferable item today for a manufacturer, and the commodity is almost incidental to the point. The finding is that the losses which actually materialise in concentrated-resource regions arrive without physical damage, while property and business interruption cover, including contingent business interruption, is built on physical damage as its trigger. That is a structural gap rather than a wording oversight. It is worth being precise about what fills it, because the products are narrower than they sound: political risk and contract frustration policies respond to specified political perils on named contracts or assets, and non-damage supply-chain products exist but are limited in availability and appetite. Trade credit is often mentioned in this context and is largely beside the point — it protects receivables against a buyer failing to pay, not the insured's own loss when a supplier stops shipping. So the question is which critical inputs come from a small number of politically concentrated sources, and what, if anything, would respond when that supply stops for a reason other than a fire. Expect the honest answer to include contractual remedies, dual sourcing and inventory alongside insurance. Note the source is a broker that sells these products.

16

Human error, not AI agents, is driving cyber losses so far in 2026

Risk & Insurance, citing cyber insurer Resilience · August 12 · Single-carrier claims data

Resilience's claims analysis, covering January 2024 to June 2026 across a portfolio of mid-size to large enterprises averaging $2.04bn in revenue, found social engineering accounted for 85.3% of incurred cyber losses in H1 2026, up from 17.7% in H1 2024, with no losses attributed to AI-specific attack vectors. Extortion produced 73% of incurred losses from just 5.8% of claims. Average claim severity fell to about $470,000 while frequency rose to 45.9 claims per 100 policies.

Why it matters — one insurer's book rather than the market, and a carrier publishing claims data is also marketing, but the direction is credible and it cuts against the prevailing narrative. Read the two headline percentages as overlapping rather than additive: they describe the same losses cut two ways, social engineering being how the attacker got in and extortion what the loss turned into, which is why they sum past 100%. "Incurred" also includes case reserves, so these are not settled payments. Two responses follow rather than one. On frequency, this is a controls and training problem, and the control most directly aimed at how these losses start is verification of payment instructions and access changes through a second, known channel — cheap, unpopular with finance teams, and repeatedly proven. On severity, it is a limits and retention question, since the value is concentrated in a small number of extortion events. That no losses at all were coded to AI attack vectors is worth putting to anyone selling on that basis, with the caveat the data itself invites: novel techniques are frequently recorded under conventional categories rather than counted separately, so this is weak evidence of absence.

17

Ohio lightning strike puts a $1.65bn claims trend back in focus

Insurance Business America, citing the Insurance Information Institute · August 13 · Trade-body data · News hook commercial, data homeowners

A lightning strike at an Ohio correctional facility prompted the coverage, but the figures cited are from the homeowners market and the two should not be conflated: US insurers paid an estimated $1.65bn in lightning-related homeowners claims in 2025, up 59% from $1.04bn in 2024, with average claim cost rising 42.8% to $26,616 — a 146.9% increase in average claim size since 2017, on Insurance Information Institute figures.

Why it matters — the data is personal lines and comes from an industry trade body, so it is directional rather than a commercial benchmark, and the reason to carry it is the shape of the trend rather than the dollar figures. Average claim size is rising far faster than claim count, which is the signature of a loss whose cost is increasingly electronic rather than structural. Whether that same driver applies at a plant is a reasonable inference rather than something these figures establish, but the exposure is real and larger in kind, since programmable controllers, drives, networked sensors and control systems are what a surge event destroys, and their replacement lead times drive a business interruption loss that can dwarf the equipment cost. The review this argues for spans several policies, because lightning damage falls across them: direct strike and resulting fire usually sit in property, mechanical and electrical failure in equipment breakdown, and a utility outage in service interruption cover, each with its own deductible, waiting period and exclusions. The two questions for a broker and for engineering are whether surge protection at panel and equipment level has been reviewed since the control systems were last upgraded, and how those policies fit together on business interruption rather than repair cost alone. Given this week's storm outbreak, that is timely rather than theoretical.

Renew Risk launches an interconnector risk model built with Aviva

CIR · August 11 · Model launch

Renew Risk released an Interconnector Risk Module, developed with Aviva, assessing subsea electricity interconnectors on route length, burial depth, seabed conditions, installation method and location, with interconnector exposure added to its industry exposure database. It extends the firm's UK, Ireland and European windstorm models. The firm's chief executive said interconnectors are among Europe's most important energy assets yet many are priced using models never designed for their complexity.

Why it matters — a specialist launch with no direct industrial application, carried for the pattern rather than the product. Modelling is what allows an exposure to be priced at all: perils that cannot be modelled tend to be written with heavy caution, wide exclusions or not at all. The honest version of that observation cuts both ways — better modelling can also reveal concentration and lead underwriters to offer less, so a new model is not automatically more capacity. The usable point for a buyer is narrower and still worth having: where a broker reports that capacity is unavailable for an unusual exposure, it is worth asking whether the constraint is the absence of a credible model rather than a settled lack of appetite, and worth asking again periodically, because that answer changes. The energy-infrastructure angle is a second-order interest for any energy-intensive manufacturer, since interconnector reliability sits upstream of industrial power prices.

19

Blenheim launches new-build vessel delivery cover

CIR · August 11 · Product launch

Blenheim Partnerships, part of the White Bear Group, launched cover protecting vessel owners against financial loss from late or failed new-build ship deliveries, including increased replacement costs, lost charter income, financing expenses, contractual liabilities and the cost of securing alternative tonnage. The underwriter said the product answers a gap in protection against the financial consequences of newbuilding delivery failures.

Why it matters — marine, and carried because the underlying idea is one manufacturers rarely insure. This is delivery-failure cover: protection against a supplier not delivering a major capital asset on time, paying the financial consequences without requiring physical damage. It is the same structural point the WTW item makes, approached from the product side. Any business with long-lead capital equipment on order carries that exposure and typically manages it only through contractual liquidated damages, which may or may not be capped below the true cost of a delayed line depending on how the contract was negotiated — worth checking rather than assuming. One distinction to carry into the broker conversation, because the terms get used interchangeably and are not the same thing: conventional delay-in-start-up or advanced loss of profits cover generally attaches to a project policy and still requires insured physical loss or damage to the equipment, so it does not answer simple supplier non-performance. Non-delivery for commercial or financial reasons is more often handled through surety and performance bonds, parent guarantees or letters of credit, with bespoke non-damage products like this one a newer and narrower option. Worth asking which of those is actually available for major equipment orders, and at what cost.
