CFO Brief — three takeaways, written to be read aloud
  1. Governance — securities class action filings rose again in the first half, and AI disclosure is now a visible strand within them. Cornerstone Research and the Stanford Law School Securities Class Action Clearinghouse counted 121 filings in the first half of 2026 against 93 in the second half of 2025, with core filings at 117 versus a historical average of 97. Fifteen filings were AI-related, and those fifteen accounted for 73% of the market-capitalisation losses the study tracks. Two cautions before this is repeated. The study does not establish that AI disclosure caused the increase — fifteen of 121 filings is a strand, not the driver — and the loss indices are measures of share-price decline around a disclosure, not of insured loss, economic damage or anything a D&O policy would pay. What follows is modest and practical: expect underwriters to ask more at the next D&O renewal about what the company has said publicly regarding its AI capabilities and governance, and make sure whoever answers that question has read the public statements.
  2. Market — the reinsurance market looks likely to stay competitive into 2027, which is a helpful backdrop for property renewals but not a promise about ours. AM Best projects that competitive pressure will rise further next year as capital continues to build, with AM Best and Guy Carpenter putting reinsurance capital at a projected $705bn by year-end 2026, roughly $130bn of that third-party and insurance-linked capital, and further rate declines likely in the absence of a major catastrophe. That is a rating agency's forward opinion rather than a measured result. It also sits one level upstream of us: reinsurance conditions influence what primary carriers can offer, but the pass-through to any individual manufacturer depends on catastrophe exposure, property valuations, fire protection and loss history, and a poor loss year at one of our sites will outweigh a soft treaty market. Casualty is moving the other way — broker commentary this summer continues to point to social inflation driving general liability and commercial auto severity — so a softening property market and a hardening casualty market can arrive in the same renewal.
  3. Exposure — organised cargo theft on the rail network is now large enough to be a programme question rather than a security nuisance. Insurance Journal reports roughly 75,000 thefts across US freight rail in 2025 and about $200m of goods stolen a year, quadrupled from pre-2020 levels, on figures from the industry group representing the major carriers including CSX, BNSF and Union Pacific — a trade association's own data, and it follows the separate Verisk CargoNet figures this brief carried on 14 August. The pattern in the cited cases is high-value consumer and tool goods taken in volume near depots and yards. The question worth putting to the broker is not whether cargo is insured but on what terms: how in-transit values are set against current replacement cost, what the deductible looks like against a pattern of frequent moderate losses rather than one large one, whether any theft or security warranty applies, and how accumulation at terminals and yards is treated when many shipments sit in one place overnight.

01

AI-related lawsuits drive sharp rise in securities class action filings

Risk & Insurance, citing Cornerstone Research and the Stanford Securities Class Action Clearinghouse · August 18 · Research report

The 2026 Midyear Assessment of Securities Class Action Filings counted 121 filings in the first half of 2026, up from 93 in the second half of 2025, with core filings at 117 against a historical average of 97. Fifteen filings were AI-related and represented 73% of the market-capitalisation losses tracked in the study. The Disclosure Dollar Loss Index stood at $529bn for the half and the Maximum Dollar Loss Index at $1,858bn. AI-related filings clustered in the Ninth Circuit with six, followed by the Second and Third Circuits with three each.

Why it matters — the headline overstates the causal link and it is worth separating what the data show from what they suggest. Filings rose, and AI-related cases are a large share of the tracked loss value; fifteen cases out of 121 is not the same as AI driving the increase, and the study does not attempt that attribution. Be careful with the loss figures too: the Disclosure Dollar Loss and Maximum Dollar Loss indices measure share-price decline around a disclosure, and they are deliberately broad measures of market-capitalisation movement rather than estimates of recoverable damages, settlement value or insured loss. The practical consequence sits at renewal rather than in the courtroom. Where a company has made public statements about AI capability, deployment or governance, expect D&O underwriters to ask what internal process stands behind those statements — the same question they learned to ask about cyber and about ESG claims. For a private or subsidiary operation the securities exposure itself does not apply, but the underlying discipline does: the marketing description of what a system does should match what the engineers say it does.

02

Hackers 'learn in real time' as agentic AI drives 36% uptick in found vulnerabilities

Insurance Journal, citing Beazley Security; also reported by CIR · August 18 · Carrier-affiliated research

Analysis from Beazley Security attributes a 36% quarter-on-quarter rise in newly disclosed vulnerabilities in the second quarter of 2026 to the adoption of agentic AI in vulnerability research. The reporting notes that discovery is currently outpacing exploitation — more weaknesses are being found than are being turned into working attacks. CIR carried the same research on the same day.

Why it matters — this is research published by a security arm of an insurer, so read it as informed and interested rather than neutral. Taken at face value, the operational point is about tempo rather than about any new class of threat: if disclosures rise by a third in a quarter, the interval between a vulnerability becoming public and a patch being applied is the exposure, and that interval is a function of patching capacity rather than of security spend. That lands hardest on plant environments, where operational technology cannot always be patched on the IT schedule and downtime for maintenance has to be scheduled against production. Two things follow. Underwriters are increasingly asking about patch cadence and about which systems are exempt from it, and the honest answer for most manufacturers involves a list of machines that cannot be taken down — which is a conversation better had before the renewal questionnaire than on it. And where patching genuinely is not possible, the compensating controls — segmentation between plant and corporate networks, monitored remote access, tested recovery — are what the underwriter is actually buying.

03

Reinsurance competitive pressures to rise in 2027, discipline can keep returns attractive: AM Best

Artemis, citing AM Best · August 18 · Rating agency outlook

AM Best projects that competitive pressures will rise further in 2027 as the reinsurance market continues to build its capital base, with capital accumulating faster than it can be deployed. AM Best and Guy Carpenter project reinsurance capital reaching $705bn by year-end 2026, of which roughly $130bn is third-party and insurance-linked capital. The agency expects underwriting results to deteriorate between year-end 2025 and 2026 and further rate declines to continue absent major catastrophe activity, describing this as a normalisation from the exceptional profitability of 2023 to 2025, and says well-disciplined participants can still generate attractive returns.

Why it matters — a rating agency's forward-looking opinion, not a measured market result, and it describes the reinsurance market rather than the market we buy in. The link between the two is real but indirect: cheaper and more plentiful reinsurance lowers the cost of the capacity primary carriers deploy, which over time tends to show up as more competitive property terms. It does not arrive uniformly. Whether it reaches a particular manufacturer depends on how the account looks to the underwriter — catastrophe exposure by location, the quality of the fire protection and engineering, whether stated values are credible, and recent loss experience — and a single significant loss will outweigh the treaty market entirely. The useful posture is to treat a softening reinsurance market as an opportunity to test the programme rather than as a discount to expect: it is the environment in which a well-prepared submission, a marketing exercise or a structural change such as a higher retention in exchange for broader terms is most likely to be rewarded.

04

Why Hurricane Lala could create a claims puzzle for brokers

Insurance Business America · August 18 · Catastrophe · Development on Edition 014

Following Hurricane Lala's passage near Hawaii's Big Island, Insurance Business reports that the overlapping hazards the storm produced — wind alongside flash flooding and mudslides — will make early documentation and prompt notification unusually important, because separating which peril caused which damage determines which policy and which deductible responds.

Why it matters — a development on the Lala item this brief carried yesterday, and the new content is the claims-handling point rather than the meteorology. Where a single event produces several perils that are covered differently — named windstorm on a percentage deductible, flood on a separate sub-limit, earth movement possibly excluded — the allocation between them is not a formality; it can be the largest number in the claim. Mudslide is the classic difficulty, since depending on wording it can fall under flood, under earth movement, or outside both. Two habits protect the position and neither is expensive: document conditions and damage early and with timestamps, before remediation changes the evidence, and give notice promptly under every policy that could conceivably respond rather than the one that seems most likely, because late notice is a defence that does not care which peril turns out to have been the right answer. Anti-concurrent-causation language, where a programme contains it, can decide the allocation before the facts are argued, which is a reason to know whether it is there in advance.

06

Insurance considerations for companies facing social media harm litigation

Risk Management Magazine (RIMS) · August 11 · Practitioner guidance · 7 days old

Writing in RIMS' magazine, the authors examine the insurance and contractual strategies available as "addictive design" litigation — claims that a product was deliberately designed to compel compulsive use — expands beyond social media platforms into other corporate sectors.

Why it matters — carried at a week old because it is the only litigation-trend item this week with a coverage analysis attached, and because the mechanism travels further than the defendants so far have. The theory reframes a design choice as the wrongful act, which is a different shape from an ordinary product claim about a defect or a failure to warn. That difference is where the coverage questions sit, and they need stating precisely rather than as a general instruction to check the wording. A commercial general liability policy responds to bodily injury and property damage caused by an occurrence, so a claim framed around psychological or behavioural harm from an intended design feature has to clear both the definition of bodily injury and the requirement that the injury was neither expected nor intended — before any product or media exclusion is reached. Public-company D&O is a poorer fit than it first appears, because entity cover is commonly limited to securities claims, so a consumer or governmental design-defect suit against the company may not trigger it at all even where the individual directors are protected. For a manufacturer the reason to follow this is the direction of travel rather than today's defendants: connected and app-controlled consumer products are where a design-intent theory would arrive next, and the answer to "which policy responds" should be established while it is hypothetical.

CatIQ raises Ontario & Quebec thunderstorm insured market loss estimate 17.5% to C$439m

Artemis, citing CatIQ · August 18 · Loss estimate revision

CatIQ, the Canadian catastrophe loss tracker, raised its insured market loss estimate for the recent Ontario and Quebec severe thunderstorm event by 17.5%, to C$439m.

Why it matters — the number matters less than the direction and the fact that it moved. Severe convective storm losses characteristically develop upward after the event as claims are reported and adjusted, which is why an initial industry estimate is a starting point rather than a measurement, and a 17.5% upward revision is an ordinary rather than a remarkable amount of development. This sits in the alternative-risk section because index-based loss estimates of exactly this kind are what industry-loss warranties and some parametric and insurance-linked structures key their triggers to, so how an estimate develops is not academic — it can determine whether a trigger attaches. For a buyer considering any index-linked cover, that is the practical question to ask alongside the trigger level: which index, measured by whom, at what point after the event, and what happens if the estimate is revised after a payment decision has been made. On our own account the relevance is thematic rather than geographic — this was a Canadian event and says nothing about Midwest storm frequency — but severe convective storm is the peril whose aggregate cost has grown most consistently, and it typically reaches a manufacturer through hail damage to roofs, rooftop plant and vehicles rather than through anything dramatic.

09

The great modern train robbery: thieves steal $200 million a year

Insurance Journal, citing the freight rail industry group · August 17 · Trade association data

Roughly 75,000 thefts occurred on US freight rail networks in 2025, amounting to about $200m in stolen goods annually and quadrupled from pre-2020 levels, on figures from the industry group representing major carriers including CSX, BNSF Railway and Union Pacific. Cited incidents include a January theft of about 1,985 pairs of unreleased Nike shoes worth more than $440,000, and a Los Angeles raid that recovered $1.5m of stolen cargo including Milwaukee Tool goods. Thefts are concentrated near depots and yards.

Why it matters — the source is the rail industry's own trade association, which has an interest in the scale of the problem being understood, and the figures should be read alongside rather than instead of the Verisk CargoNet data this brief carried on 14 August. The exposure is real regardless of who counts it, and it has a specific shape: frequent, moderate-value losses concentrated at fixed points in the network rather than rare catastrophic ones. That shape is what makes it a programme question. A deductible calibrated to a single large loss can leave a year of thefts almost entirely uninsured in aggregate, which is the failure mode worth checking. Alongside it: whether in-transit values reflect current replacement cost rather than a schedule set years ago; whether the cargo cover contains a theft or security warranty, and if so whether the actual routing complies with it; how accumulation is treated when many shipments sit in one yard overnight, since that is where the concentration risk lives; and, on inbound freight, who bears the risk of loss under the shipping terms and what the carrier's own liability is limited to, because carrier liability limits are routinely far below the value of the goods.

2026 manufacturing mid-year state of the market

Why it matters — this is a broker's own market report, which means it is well-informed about placements and also written by a firm that sells the reviews it recommends; the observations are worth more than the advice. It is carried at three weeks old because one of the four points is the most concrete renewal issue on this list. PFAS exclusions are broadening across casualty and environmental wordings, and unlike most coverage debates this one has a hard edge: an absolute PFAS exclusion is not a limitation to negotiate around at claim time, it is the difference between a defended claim and an undefended one. The state obligations should not be treated as a single deadline — they differ by state in scope, in whether they impose reporting or an outright product restriction, and in effective date, so the applicability question is specific to which products contain what and where they are sold. The two questions worth putting to the broker before the next casualty renewal are what PFAS language currently sits in each policy, and whether the exclusion as drafted reaches components and materials purchased from suppliers rather than only substances we knowingly use.
