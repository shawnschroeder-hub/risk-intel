CFO Brief — three takeaways, written to be read aloud
  1. Exposure — the tariff pause is three days, not a settlement, and the supply-chain rerouting already under way is a contingent business interruption question rather than a business income one. A three-day pause on 50% tariffs covering roughly $20bn of Canadian imports, delayed from an 19 August deadline while negotiations continue; the Canadian Prime Minister said substantial progress had been made but important work remained. Nationwide's commentary describes the shift as expecting uncertainty and taking a deeper look at material supplies and supply chains, with inventories tight enough that a demand spike could leave product unavailable. The insurance consequence needs stating precisely, because it is easy to get wrong: a supplier move does not lengthen our own period of restoration, which is governed by the time to repair or replace our own damaged property. What it changes is dependent-property cover. Contingent business interruption generally responds to physical damage at a supplier's location, and often only at scheduled or named suppliers — or on a blanket basis that is sublimited and may be restricted to direct suppliers. A supplier switched for tariff reasons can therefore fall outside the schedule without anyone noticing. The action is to reconcile the contingent BI supplier list against the current supplier map, and separately to look at extra expense if longer lead times would delay a return to full output after a loss.
  2. Market — cyber risk is consolidating, and is being re-drawn as an accumulation problem that crosses policy lines. The version worth putting to a broker is narrower — if a single cloud or software vendor fails and nobody is hacked, does anything in the tower respond to the operational loss? This is most directly a vehicle, trailer and component exposure; plan the next casualty renewal on the environment as it stands.

01

Munich Re acquires cyber insurtech At-Bay for $575 million

Insurance Journal; also Insurance Business America · August 19 · M&A

Munich Re agreed to acquire At-Bay for $575m, expected to close in the first quarter of 2027 subject to regulatory approvals. At-Bay writes cyber insurance and provides managed detection and response services, primarily for small and mid-sized US businesses, with about $278m of gross written premium and roughly 280 staff across the US and Israel. Hartford Steam Boiler, part of Munich Re's Global Specialty Insurance division, will oversee the business. Munich Re board member Mike Kerner said it is expected to "evolve into a strong earnings growth driver over time," and At-Bay chief executive Rotem Iram framed the deal as helping close the protection gap for smaller businesses.

Why it matters — a reinsurer buying a primary cyber writer that runs its own security monitoring is a bet that cyber underwriting and active risk control are fundamentally the same business rather than two services sold alongside each other. It would be an extrapolation from a single transaction to conclude that carriers generally will start requiring network visibility as a condition of capacity, and that inference is not drawn here. The narrower and safer reading is that a major reinsurer has decided monitoring data improves cyber underwriting enough to pay $575m for it, and that the direction of travel is worth watching. The practical step is unglamorous: establish what monitoring, endpoint or reporting conditions already sit inside the current cyber policy and its subjectivities, because those conditions are easier to negotiate before a renewal than to discover during a claim.

02

Tariff pause is temporary — the exposure shift for clients isn't

Insurance Business America, citing Nationwide · August 19 · Carrier commentary

A three-day pause on 50% tariffs covering roughly $20bn of Canadian imports, delayed from an 19 August deadline while the US and Canada work toward a trade deal; Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney said substantial progress had been made but that important work remained. Nationwide's Kristina Talkowski said it "has really become about expecting uncertainty and taking a deeper look at material supplies and supply chains." Colleague Ben Ayers noted that inventories have become tight in many industries and that businesses have largely absorbed tariff costs rather than passing them on, and that if demand picks up "you might not have all the product available for your end client."

Why it matters — the tariff is a cost and margin question rather than an insurance one, and the insurance consequence is both real and narrower than it first appears. It is tempting to say that a more distant supplier with longer lead times extends the period of restoration under a business income policy. That is not how the cover works. The period of restoration is measured by the time it should reasonably take to repair, rebuild or replace the insured's own damaged property, and it is not extended because raw material now takes longer to arrive in the ordinary course. The exposure that genuinely shifts sits in dependent-property cover. Contingent business interruption normally responds to physical loss or damage at a supplier's premises, and frequently only at suppliers that are named or scheduled; where blanket unnamed cover exists it is typically sublimited and may be confined to direct suppliers rather than reaching down the chain. A supplier changed for tariff reasons can therefore drop outside the schedule with no one noticing until a claim. Two actions follow, and they are different actions: reconcile the dependent-property schedule against the current supplier map, and look separately at extra expense cover if longer lead times would delay a return to full output after a loss.

03

Gallagher Re creates digital risk unit as AI reshapes accumulation

Insurance Business America, citing Gallagher Re (reinsurance broker) · August 19 · Broker practice launch

Gallagher Re has consolidated AI liability, data centres, cyber and digital risk engineering into a single advisory function operating across property, casualty and specialty classes. Ian Newman becomes global head of digital risk alongside his existing cyber role, with Freddie Scarratt leading AI liability and Luca Drane leading data centres. Newman said clients and markets are increasingly seeking guidance on AI and digital dependency "not only within cyber but across a wide range of business lines." The unit cites the 2024 CrowdStrike outage at roughly $5.4bn of damage to Fortune 500 companies against $300m to $1.5bn insured.

Why it matters — this is a broker building a billable practice, so the framing is commercial and should be read that way. The underlying observation still holds and is testable on any programme, but it needs one correction to the way it is usually told. A single technology failure does not automatically trigger property, casualty and cyber policies at the same time: property cover ordinarily requires physical loss or damage, and casualty requires a third-party claim, so an operational loss with neither may find nothing to attach to. That is the actual point rather than an objection to it. Policies bought separately, from different markets, on different wordings, are not designed to sit alongside one another, and the gap that opens is between them rather than inside any one of them. The CrowdStrike shortfall is therefore mostly a question about what non-damage business interruption is covered anywhere in the tower — not about whether the limits are large enough. The useful exercise is a single named scenario, walked through every policy: a critical vendor fails for forty-eight hours, nobody is hacked, no property is damaged, and production stops. Which policy responds, at what retention, after what waiting period, and what does it exclude?

04

Why your carrier's storm model vintage matters at renewal

Insurance Business America, citing Karen Clark & Company (catastrophe modelling vendor), broker Aon, trade body Triple-I, rating agency Moody's and broker Gallagher Re · August 19 · Catastrophe modelling

Severe convective storm produced $61bn of insured losses globally in 2025 and $51bn in the United States, a third consecutive year above $50bn, making it the costliest insured peril of the century so far on the figures cited. The article argues that traditional statistical catastrophe models are poorly suited to severe convective storm relative to newer physical and AI-informed approaches. Karen Clark & Company ingests more than 30 gigabytes of satellite, radar and weather data daily and has archived over 100 terabytes since 2018. Moody's found nearly half of properties showed modelled loss changes exceeding 15% after AI data enrichment. Gallagher Re research attributes 80–90% of the long-term increase in US severe convective storm insured losses to economic and societal factors.

Why it matters — the Gallagher Re finding is the most important number in the piece, and it does not contradict the headline so much as separate two things that are easy to conflate. Exposure growth, higher property values and repair-cost inflation explain most of the historical rise in losses; hazard modelling governs how that risk is priced, allocated and accumulated. Improving the model changes the price and the spread of the risk, not the frequency of the storms. Both matter at renewal for a multi-site manufacturer in the Midwest, because two carriers looking at identical properties can reach materially different views on model vintage alone — though it would be an overstatement to say quotes diverge purely on that basis, since appetite, reinsurance cost, attachment point, engineering data and loss history all move the number too. The controllable input is exposure data quality. A model is only as good as what is fed into it, and that means more than the statement of values: geocoding accuracy, construction class, occupancy, roof age and the secondary characteristics that hail and wind models are most sensitive to. A property schedule that has not been revisited in three years is the single most likely reason a quote comes back worse than expected.

05

Regulator objections quantified: ZestyAI says 44% of filings have at least one

Insurance Journal, citing ZestyAI's "Approval Velocity 2026" report (analytics vendor) · August 19 · Vendor research

ZestyAI analysed more than 20,000 approved rate filings across all 50 states and the District of Columbia over the twelve months ending 8 May 2026. Some 44%, or 8,776 filings, drew at least one regulator objection, which was associated with a median of 38 additional days before approval. Objection rates ran highest in homeowners at 53.2% and personal auto at 50.7%, and lowest in commercial property at 34.3%. Wisconsin recorded the fastest approval times across all lines, while California and New York were among the slowest. ZestyAI's Bryan Rehor argued that every month a filing sits in objection is a month a carrier writes business at a rate it has determined is insufficient.

Why it matters — a vendor's own report, published by a firm that sells analytics to the carriers making these filings, though the underlying filings are public record and the methodology is at least stated. Two limitations belong on the item. The study covers approved filings only, so it says nothing about filings that were withdrawn, rejected or still pending, and those are precisely the cases where regulatory friction bites hardest; and the median 38 days is an association between objection and delay rather than a measured causal effect against a matched counterfactual. Commercial property shows the lowest objection rate of the three lines reported, which is what you would expect given that commercial lines face lighter rate regulation than personal lines in most states. That also limits how far the finding travels: large-account industrial business is frequently written on negotiated, manuscript or non-filed forms where this process does not apply at all. Read it as a description of the regulatory tempo sitting behind carrier pricing decisions rather than as anything that describes a corporate renewal directly.

Noted for the record because reinsurance market structure is worth tracking over years rather than quarters, and because a carrier's reinsurance arrangements are what ultimately determine the capacity it can offer on a large industrial risk.

07

New bill aims to defuse nuclear verdicts

Land Line · August 18 · Legislation · introduced only, no vote in either chamber

Senator Deb Fischer (R-Neb.) and Representative Mike Flood (R-Neb.) have introduced the Uniform Vehicle Safety Standards Act, S.5366 in the Senate and H.R.10050 in the House. The bill would provide that compliance with federal motor vehicle safety standards set by the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration precludes manufacturer liability under state common law, establishing a uniform national standard in place of what the sponsors describe as a patchwork of state rules. The stated impetus is a Missouri jury verdict of $462m against trailer manufacturer Wabash arising from a crash that killed two people, in which the trailer met all applicable federal safety standards; the award was subsequently reduced, by an amount the reporting does not specify. Flood said that "we've seen a sharp rise in lawsuits awarding damages based on state standards."

Why it matters — status before substance. The bill has been introduced in both chambers and nothing more: no committee vote, no floor action, no enactment, and federal preemption of state tort law is contested legal ground rather than a technicality that follows automatically from a statute. Two things about the verdict also need stating carefully. The $462m is the original jury award and not the operative judgment, and the reporting gives neither the reduced figure nor the post-trial or appellate position, so it should be used as an indicator of jury behaviour rather than as a measure of anyone's exposure. And the widely repeated lesson — that regulatory compliance was not a defence — is stronger than the facts support. Compliance remains evidence and a substantial defence in product litigation; what these facts show is that it was not a complete shield before this jury. The distinction matters because the practical response differs. If compliance were worthless, nothing could be done; because it is necessary but not sufficient, the defensible position is documented decision-making about known hazards beyond the minimum standard — what alternatives were considered, what was rejected, and the reasoning at the time. That file is what a plaintiff's expert will attack and what defence counsel will need. Most directly relevant to vehicle, trailer, component and fleet exposures rather than to manufacturers generally.

08

Litigation Funding Transparency Act of 2026 (S.3826) — status unchanged

Congress.gov; introduction announced by the Institute for Legal Reform (tort-reform advocacy body) · introduced February 2026 · pending in the Senate Judiciary Committee

The bill, introduced in February 2026 by Senate Judiciary Committee chairman Chuck Grassley with Senators Thom Tillis, John Kennedy and John Cornyn, would require disclosure of outside investors in federal class actions and multidistrict litigation, restrict funders from controlling legal strategy, and bar their access to confidential discovery material. It applies to class actions, multidistrict litigation and large coordinated federal proceedings involving one hundred or more cases. It remains pending in the Senate Judiciary Committee, with no committee vote, no floor action and no enactment.

Why it matters — carried as a standing status line rather than as news, because the absence of movement is itself the information and this brief has now reported the same position for several weeks. Nothing in the third-party litigation funding environment has changed, and the federal disclosure regime the bill would create does not exist. Two qualifications keep the item honest. First, the bill reaches federal aggregate litigation only: class actions, MDLs and large coordinated proceedings. Ordinary single-plaintiff product liability suits brought in state court — the setting for a great many large verdicts against manufacturers — would be untouched by it even if it passed tomorrow. Second, the absence of a federal statute does not mean no disclosure obligation exists anywhere; several states have enacted their own funding disclosure requirements, some federal districts have local rules or standing orders, and an individual judge can order disclosure through case management. The practical position for a defendant is therefore jurisdiction-specific, and it is a question for outside counsel in the venue rather than one that a federal bill's progress answers.

09

AIG starts parametric cloud outage solution, working with Parametrix

Insurance Journal; announced by AIG · announced August 13, reported August 14 · 6 days old, still relevant

AIG has launched a parametric cloud outage solution in the United States, available as an endorsement for eligible AIG cyber clients. The trigger relies on Parametrix's monitoring of cloud service availability, with the product positioned as a response to business interruption arising from third-party cloud service failures rather than from an attack on the insured's own systems.

Why it matters — the clearest current example of parametric cover filling a gap rather than replacing an existing one, provided the gap is described accurately. It is not the case that traditional cyber business interruption requires a security failure before it responds: many current cyber wordings do cover non-malicious system failure and dependent system failure. The practical obstacles to recovering a short third-party outage are more mundane — the waiting period, which is often eight to twelve hours and can exceed the outage itself; the sublimit that typically applies to dependent business interruption; and the difficulty of proving and quantifying a loss over a brief window. A parametric endorsement pays when the specified index condition is met, which removes the loss-adjustment argument almost entirely. What it introduces in exchange is basis risk: the payout tracks the measured index rather than the actual loss, so it can pay when there was little damage and pay too little when there was a great deal. Three questions decide whether it is worth anything in a manufacturing context. Which specific cloud services and regions does the monitoring actually cover? Do the systems that stop production — MES, ERP, warehouse management, logistics scheduling — genuinely sit on those services, or on a private or hybrid platform outside the index? And how does a parametric payout interact with recovery under the underlying cyber policy, since two recoveries for one loss is not usually how these are structured.

10

What risk managers need to know about the EU AI Act and evolving AI regulation

Risk Management Magazine (RIMS) · August 19 · Practitioner guidance

On this publication's account, bans on certain prohibited AI practices and transparency requirements are already in effect; obligations for "high-risk" AI systems, originally due in August 2026, have been postponed to December 2027; California risk assessment obligations begin on 1 January 2027, with certification and reporting to the California Privacy Protection Agency from 2028. The article argues companies should use the delay rather than wait it out, noting that organisations typically need 12 to 24 months to build a compliant governance framework, and recommends risk assessments to determine whether systems qualify as high-risk, cross-functional governance teams spanning legal, product, engineering and cybersecurity, careful documentation of intended uses, product safety-style controls including testing datasets and logging and human oversight, and attention to US litigation risk around customer-facing AI.

Why it matters — the timetable should be treated as this publication's summary rather than as verified statute. The AI Act applies different dates to different categories of high-risk system, and any postponement needs to be traced to the legal instrument that enacted it before a compliance plan is built on it; a proposal or a policy announcement to delay is not a change in the law. What is not in doubt is that the prohibitions and the transparency duties already apply, so a delay to the high-risk tier does not clear the deck. For a manufacturer, the live exposure is rarely the customer-facing chatbot that dominates the general commentary. It is AI embedded in a product sold into the EU, or in safety-relevant process control, which is exactly where the high-risk classification bites and where the documentation burden is heaviest. The most avoidable own goal is the point about marketing materials: regulators assess a system's intended purpose partly from how it is described in sales collateral, contracts and demonstrations, and that copy is typically written a long way from legal or risk. A capability claim drafted to win a tender can determine a regulatory classification that the engineering team never anticipated.

Employers tighten benefits cost oversight as medical and pharmacy pressures mount

Risk & Insurance, citing Gallagher's U.S. Benefits Strategy & Benchmarking Survey (broker research) · August 17 · 2 days old, still relevant

Some 36% of employers reported premium increases of 10% or more at their most recent renewal, and 78% anticipate higher health care costs in 2026. 73% cite managing medical service costs as a top concern, while only 50% believe their organisation manages health care costs effectively. 45% now self-insure the medical plan with the highest enrolment, up three points from 2025. On pharmacy, 20% conduct a regular PBM request for proposal every three years and 22% cover GLP-1 drugs for weight loss. Longer-term benefit design continues to broaden, with 28% offering long-term care insurance, up five points from 2023, and 36% offering pet insurance, up thirteen points.

Why it matters — a broker's own benchmarking survey with self-selected respondents, so the direction of travel is worth more than the levels. The pharmacy finding is the one that justifies a question, but it needs stating carefully: that only a fifth run a PBM request for proposal on a three-year cycle does not establish that everyone else leaves pharmacy pricing unexamined. Others may review annually, on a different cycle, through a consultant, or inside a bundled arrangement where the testing happens elsewhere. What the figure does support is a narrow and answerable question — when was our own PBM arrangement last market-tested, on what basis, and by whom — at a time when pharmacy is consistently identified as a leading cost driver. The gap between 73% calling cost management a top concern and 50% believing they do it well is the other honest signal here. This is employee benefits rather than property and casualty, a different budget with a different owner in most organisations, and it is included because it competes for the same total cost-of-risk attention at CFO level rather than because it touches the insurance programme.

12

Data center gas plants to boost US power emissions by 20%

Insurance Journal / Bloomberg, citing BloombergNEF (energy research firm) · August 19 · Research

BloombergNEF tracks 99 proposed natural gas plants supporting data centre development, representing 126 gigawatts of planned on-site generation capacity across 22 states with more than a third of it in Texas. At industry-standard emissions rates those plants would produce roughly 318m metric tons of CO2 annually, equivalent to about a 20% increase in US power sector emissions. Amazon and Microsoft's combined West Texas facilities alone could generate approximately 45m metric tons a year. David Pomerantz of the Energy and Policy Institute described "immense pressure on the whole sector to get power, and get it fast."

Why it matters — proposed capacity is not built capacity. Announcement pipelines of this kind routinely shrink as permitting, turbine supply, financing and local opposition take their toll, so the 20% figure is best read as an upper bound on an announced pipeline rather than a forecast of what will be emitted. It is carried for one second-order effect that genuinely reaches a manufacturer: if the national power-sector emissions baseline rises, a supply-chain or Scope 3 emissions commitment becomes harder to hold without doing anything differently, and how much harder depends on procurement contracts, geography and the accounting methodology in use. One argument is deliberately not made here. It would be easy to claim that new industrial loads of this scale crowd out manufacturers in grid interconnection queues, but on-site generation may just as plausibly relieve pressure on the grid as add to it, so no claim is made about connection queues either way. None of this is an insurance exposure today; it is a two-to-five year planning input that belongs in the same conversation as energy procurement rather than in the renewal file.

HDI Global backs US broker push with new CEO and solid H1 results

A stated US growth push from a carrier of that shape signals appetite in roughly the right class. The concrete step is to ask the broker whether HDI is on the current submission list and, if not, why not.

Latin American P/C insurers enter softer market but earnings stay resilient

Why it matters — a rating agency's forward-looking opinion rather than a measured result, and relevant only to the extent that there are Latin American operations, subsidiaries or significant suppliers in the picture. One inference is deliberately not drawn. It would be easy to read the 3.4% penetration figure as evidence that local capacity is thin for a large industrial risk, but penetration measures premium volume against GDP and says nothing directly about the capacity any particular market can offer on a specific account.
