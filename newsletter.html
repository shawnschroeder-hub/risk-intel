No. 011 — WEDNESDAY, AUGUST 12, 2026 — 21 ITEMS — 12-SOURCE ROSTER
CFO Brief — three takeaways, written to be read aloud
  1. Market — the soft market is still running, but a major reinsurer has now said out loud that it expects the slide to slow. The Q2 index from broker Marsh, published 23 July, has global commercial rates down 6%, an eighth consecutive quarterly decline, with property down 12% and casualty up 2%. Today Hannover Re's Sven Althoff said the reinsurer expects property catastrophe price reductions to decelerate. The two measure different markets — Marsh tracks primary placements, Althoff was speaking about property cat reinsurance — so this is a signal rather than a contradiction, and reinsurance is the market that eventually sets the other. The timing implication: if the intention is to convert cheap property pricing into durable structure, the window is measured in renewals rather than years. Casualty remains the exception and is still moving against the buyer.
  2. Exposure — a third of manufacturers took a cyber hit last year, and roughly the same fraction cannot say whether their insurance would respond. Make UK's Cyber Security in Manufacturing report, published 10 August, found 30% of UK manufacturers suffered a cyber incident in the past twelve months, directly or through their supply chain, with production downtime and higher operating costs the most common consequences. The figure that should stop the room is the second: almost a third either carry no cyber insurance or are unsure whether their cover applies to cyber disruption, and only 51% hold an incident response plan. Most modern cyber policies do include dependent business interruption cover, so the gap is rarely its absence — it is its shape. Ask for the sub-limit, the waiting period, and whether the wording is restricted to named dependent providers, because a schedule listing three IT vendors will not respond when the incident hits a component supplier.
  3. Severity — there is now hard loss data on where concentrated, power-hungry assets actually fail, and it is fire. Allianz Commercial analysis of 221 data centre claims worth about €677m ($782m), reported today, found fire drove well over half the claims value while accounting for only 14% of claims by number; water damage led by frequency at 21%. Separately, reporting today describes AI facilities' volatile power draw physically damaging their own electrical equipment. The read-across is a hypothesis worth testing rather than a finding — the sample is data centres, not plants — but the shape is one most property portfolios recognise. It prompts a management-of-change question: where significant electrical load has been added to existing buildings, was the fire protection and electrical infrastructure reassessed against the new load, or is it still specified for the old one?

01

Data centre boom fuels rising fire, water and business interruption losses

Risk & Insurance, citing Allianz Commercial · August 12 · Claims study

Allianz Commercial analysed 221 data centre insurance claims totalling roughly EUR 677m ($782m). Fire accounted for well over half the total claims value despite representing only 14% of claims by number, while water damage was the most frequent cause at 21%. Europe contributed 53% of claims by number and 38% by value, and the study puts about 79% of global data centre capacity in areas of elevated natural hazard exposure.

Why it matters — the most useful loss data published this week. The shape — a low-frequency, high-severity fire exposure alongside a high-frequency, lower-severity water exposure — is one most manufacturing property portfolios recognise, and it argues for spending protection budget on the fire scenario while managing water through detection and housekeeping. The natural-hazard figure belongs in a siting discussion: concentration of critical capacity in hazard-exposed locations is a decision made by operations and paid for by insurance.

02

UK factories face rising cyber disruption risk

CIR, citing Make UK — the UK manufacturers' trade body · August 12 · Report published August 10 · Membership survey

Make UK's Cyber Security in Manufacturing report found 30% of UK manufacturers experienced a cyber incident in the past twelve months, either directly or through their supply chain. Production downtime and increased operating costs were the most common consequences; 31% of those affected reported reduced output or delays, and 23% suffered component or material shortages. Almost a third of manufacturers either hold no cyber insurance or are unsure whether their cover would respond to cyber disruption; 51% have an incident response plan.

Why it matters — the most directly actionable item today. UK data, but the exposure is structural rather than national: the supply-chain transmission route is identical in any multi-site manufacturer. Most modern cyber policies include dependent business interruption cover, so the question is its shape — the sub-limit, the waiting period, and whether it responds only for named dependent providers. Note separately that traditional property contingent business interruption generally still requires physical damage, so a cyber event stopping a line may not reach that programme at all.

Global commercial insurance rates fall 6% in Q2, an eighth consecutive quarterly decline

Global Insurance Market Index from broker Marsh, reported by Insurance Journal · July 23 · Broker-published data · Older but critical

Marsh's Q2 2026 index recorded a 6% global composite rate decline, following 5% in Q1 and marking the eighth consecutive quarter of decreases. Property rates fell 12% while casualty rose 2%, with the casualty pressure attributed largely to US conditions. Marsh cites abundant capacity, strong insurer profitability, lower reinsurance costs and higher investment returns. All regions recorded decreases: IMEA down 16%, Pacific down 13%, Latin America and Caribbean down 9%, UK down 8%, Canada down 7%, Europe down 6%, Asia down 5%.

Why it matters — carried from July because it remains the authoritative frame for every renewal conversation, and because property and casualty diverge so sharply that a blended view of "the market" is actively misleading. A 12% property fall against a 2% casualty rise means the two programmes should be negotiated on entirely different assumptions. Two caveats travel with it: this is the Q2 read rather than a fresh datapoint, and the composite is global, not US.

05

Most insurance renewal rate hikes slow: Ivans

Business Insurance, citing insurance software vendor Ivans · August 2026, day not stated · US renewal data · Surfaced via site search; direct access blocked today

Ivans renewal-rate data for July shows increases slowing across most lines: commercial auto up 4.03% against 4.58% in June, business owners policies up 5.94%, general liability up 4.99%, commercial property up 6.16%, and umbrella up 7.42% — the highest of the tracked lines but down from 7.60%. Why it matters — read alongside the Marsh index rather than instead of it, because the two disagree instructively. Marsh's index is global while Ivans is US-focused, so the 12% property fall is not a US number and should not be read as one; on top of that the two differ by account size, distribution channel and method. Both can be accurate about their own samples. The practical translation for a US plant schedule is that the headline reduction is not automatically ours to claim, and the honest planning assumption sits between the two. Umbrella at 7.42% is the number to watch, since it prices the excess layers above our primary casualty.

06

AI's volatile power demand is damaging its own data centres

Insurance Journal · August 12 · Equipment and power

Rapid swings in the power demand of AI data centres are straining and damaging vital electrical equipment.

Why it matters — a transferable engineering finding rather than a technology story. Equipment breakdown claims are driven less by total load than by the rate at which load changes, and any facility that has added variable, high-draw equipment onto infrastructure specified for a steadier profile may be carrying a version of the same exposure. For a manufacturer the parallels are large drives, induction and arc processes, and recently electrified lines. Two questions to engineering: has the electrical infrastructure been reassessed since the load profile changed, and does our equipment breakdown cover include the resulting business interruption rather than the repair alone.

07

Taylor Farms and Taco Bell face multiple lawsuits over cyclospora outbreak

Insurance Journal · August 11 · Product liability

Class-action lawsuits have been filed against Taylor Farms and Taco Bell over a nationwide cyclospora outbreak.

Why it matters — the clearest current example of how a contamination event travels through a supply chain as liability. Producer, processor and point-of-sale brand are all named, the pattern any component manufacturer should recognise, since being several steps back from the consumer does not remove you from the caption. Contractually: what indemnity do our supply agreements give against an upstream defect, and do customers hold reciprocal rights against us. On coverage, the point most often discovered too late is that a standard products liability policy responds to third-party bodily injury and property damage but excludes the cost of recalling the product itself — the sistership exclusion — so recall expense, customer economic loss and brand rehabilitation require a separate product recall or contaminated products policy. Whether we carry one, and at what limit, is a different question from whether the liability tower is adequate.

08

Litigation Funding Transparency Act of 2026 (S.3826) — status check

Congress.gov · Introduced February 2026 · Standing item · Introduced only — not law

The bill, introduced by Senators Grassley, Tillis, Kennedy and Cornyn, would require disclosure of third-party litigation funders and their funding agreements in federal class actions and multi-district litigation, require parties to state whether a funder is a foreign state or foreign-controlled entity, and bar funders from influencing litigation strategy or settlement. It remains introduced and has not passed either chamber.

Why it matters — carried as a status correction rather than as news, because this is the item most likely to be misremembered as enacted. Nothing has changed at federal statutory level. One precision worth carrying: the absence of a federal statute does not mean no disclosure duty can arise — individual judges, particular MDLs and some district local rules already require funding disclosure, so the answer in any given case is court-specific rather than uniform. The reason to track it is that a funded plaintiff can refuse a reasonable settlement and hold out for a verdict, and knowing a funder is present is what lets a defendant price that behaviour into reserving.

09

Nuclear verdict baseline — 149 verdicts totalling $25.1bn in 2025

CaseGlide, citing Litigation Sentinel tracking · 2025 full-year data · Vendor-published · Older but critical

Tracking counted 149 nuclear verdicts — awards of $10m or more — totalling $25.1bn across 28 states in 2025. A thermonuclear verdict is defined as $100m or more. The figures come from a claims-technology vendor rather than a neutral research body, so they carry a commercial interest and are carried as a baseline rather than as authority.

Why it matters — no new verdict of note surfaced in today's sweep, so this is the standing scale of the problem rather than a development. The mean works out near $168m, but a mean is the wrong tool on a distribution this skewed: a handful of very large awards drag it above the typical case, headline verdicts are frequently reduced on post-trial motion or appeal, and punitive components may be uninsurable in some states. The defensible use is directional — awards of this scale occur often enough that limit adequacy should be tested against a plausible verdict scenario rather than against our own loss history, since departing from history is precisely what these awards do.

12

Viewpoint: is it time to rethink the "secondary peril" label and reclassify risk?

Insurance Journal · August 12 · Opinion piece

An opinion piece arguing that intensifying climate events make the "secondary peril" classification — covering hail, wildfire, flood and severe convective storm — increasingly inadequate as exposure expands. Clearly labelled as the author's view rather than reporting.

Why it matters — the terminology matters more than it sounds, because it influences how much modelling attention a peril receives and therefore how well it is priced. Secondary perils are where the industry's modelling history is shortest, and they are also where the property programme's deductibles, sub-limits and percentage-of-value wordings tend to sit. Those terms are disclosed rather than hidden, but they are easy to carry forward from an older programme without anyone re-testing them. For a multi-site manufacturer the useful exercise is to look at the property schedule specifically for hail, flood and convective storm sub-limits and confirm they were set against current exposure.

13

Autonomous AI attack on Taiwan linked to China

Insurance Business America · August 12 · Cyber · Attribution is the reporting's

Reporting on an autonomous AI-driven cyber attack on Taiwan attributed to China, with coverage concerns raised as cyber policies meet a new class of threat. The attribution is the reporting's, not an established finding.

Why it matters — the coverage question is the one to focus on, and it is not the technology. A common misreading is worth correcting, because it will come up: state attribution alone does not trigger a war exclusion. Those exclusions generally turn on whether the act amounts to war or a warlike operation, and while a number of cyber wordings have added specific state-backed-attack language in recent years, the trigger and the evidential standard vary by policy. That is exactly why it is worth asking rather than assuming. The precise question: under our cyber policy, what must be established before the war or state-backed exclusion can be invoked, who carries the burden, and does the wording rely on a government's formal attribution or the insurer's own assessment.

14

FDA urges sanitation and supplier checks to curb fresh-cut produce contamination

Insurance Journal · August 12 · Regulatory guidance

The FDA finalised guidance on sanitation and supplier verification for the preparation and packaging of fresh-cut produce.

Why it matters — food manufacturing specifically, but the mechanism is general and worth naming precisely, because it is easy to overstate. FDA guidance is non-binding: it is not a regulation and does not by itself create a legal duty. What it does create is a published expectation, and a plaintiff can offer that as evidence of what a reasonable operator should have been doing. It informs the standard of care rather than setting it, and whether it carries further weight depends on the underlying statute and the jurisdiction. That is the connection to the cyclospora litigation above. Wherever a regulator finalises guidance touching our processes, documenting the gap analysis promptly is worth considerably more than assembling it after a loss.

15

Americans just endured the hottest month in 132 years

Insurance Journal · August 12 · Climate and workplace

Reporting that the month just ended was the hottest across the US mainland in 132 years of records. The reporting retrieved does not name the dataset or measuring body, so the figure is carried as reported.

Why it matters — heat is a workers' compensation and equipment exposure before it is a property one, and it is under-managed in both directions. On the comp side, the widely applied control is acclimatisation scheduling: OSHA's heat guidance and the state heat rules in California, Oregon, Washington and Minnesota all build around it, on the basis that new and returning workers carry disproportionate risk in the opening days of a heat event. On the equipment side, sustained high ambient temperatures reduce the rated capacity of electrical gear and cooling systems, which connects directly to the AI power-demand item above. For plants without air conditioning in production areas, the practical question is whether the heat plan is triggered by a forecast threshold or by someone noticing it is hot.

Declining rates widen the underinsurance gap: Marsh

Business Insurance, citing broker Marsh · August 2026, day not stated · Surfaced via site search; direct access blocked today

Marsh reporting that falling rates are widening the gap between insured values and actual replacement exposure.

Why it matters — the counterweight to every other market item today, and the one most likely to matter at the next loss. The driver of the gap is construction and equipment cost inflation, which raises replacement cost whether or not anyone updates the schedule; what a soft market removes is the underwriter pressure that would otherwise force a correction. When premium is falling, nobody is asked to justify declared values, and they quietly drift below replacement cost. The consequence appears only at claim time, as a coinsurance or margin-clause adjustment on a loss assumed fully covered. This argues for spending part of the property saving on a valuation refresh rather than banking all of it — the least glamorous and most reliably valuable use of a soft market.
