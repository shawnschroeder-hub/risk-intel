01

US widens lettuce-related cyclosporiasis probe to 20 states

Insurance Journal, with corroborating data from the CDC investigation · August 28 · Active outbreak investigation

Insurance Journal reports the outbreak linked to iceberg lettuce served at Taco Bell and sourced from Taylor Farms' operations in Mexico, with the probe widened to 20 states, recently adding Georgia, Tennessee and Texas, and two deaths in Michigan in people with significant underlying health conditions. FDA says all recalled iceberg lettuce has been removed from the market, and FDA and CDC continue onsite inspection and sampling at Mexican farms and processing facilities alongside Mexican officials. On the figures — two counts, different scopes: the CDC investigation records 10,930 illnesses from 17 states with at least 454 hospitalisations, onset dates from 14 June to 3 August 2026, and notes most illnesses began before the Taylor Farms de Mexico recall of 17 July 2026, with the recalled lettuce also reaching retail sold as Marketside at Walmart. Insurance Journal separately reports a national total of 11,458 cases and 495 hospitalisations across 20 states. These are different reporting cut-offs and state counts and should be quoted separately with their sources, not merged. A third figure in the same Insurance Journal item — 14,510 cases attributed to Michigan alone — exceeds its own stated national total and should not be quoted at all until reconciled.

Why it matters — this brief carried Taylor Farms yesterday for a Reuters finding that it filed OSHA injury reports at only five to seven of seventeen required facilities. The temptation is to join the two into a safety-culture narrative, and that overreaches: a recordkeeping failure at some facilities does not establish a companywide pattern, and prior conduct in an unrelated operational domain is not automatically admissible — propensity use is generally barred, and relevance and prejudice rulings govern. The honest framing is narrower: two adverse findings about one company in two days is a reputational and discovery problem, not an evidentiary bridge. On the insurance mechanics, bodily-injury claims from contaminated product would generally be considered under commercial general liability and the products-completed operations hazard, though response turns on occurrence definition, causation, territory — relevant here, since the product originated in Mexico — insured status, exclusions and how claims aggregate. The insured's own withdrawal costs are a separate question from third-party claims by retailers and restaurants, and a recall or contaminated-products policy responds only if its own triggers and defined costs are met. The questions worth putting to the broker are specific: how occurrence and any batch or lot wording aggregate a wave of claims, how the self-insured retention applies per claim or per occurrence, whether foreign-territory provisions reach product sourced abroad, and what vendor or additional-insured obligations run to customers. The durable operational point stands on its own: most illnesses preceded the recall, so traceability speed determines how much exposure a recall can still prevent.

02

Cursor AI used in Russia-linked hit on seven firms as insurers race to rewrite cyber cover

Insurance Business America · August 28 · Incident reporting · Seven confirmed victims, a small sample

A Russian-speaking ransomware group calling itself Aur0ra used Cursor, an AI coding assistant, in breaches between April and May 2026. Rather than exploiting a technical flaw in the tool, the attackers told it they were conducting lawful security testing and, when refused, restarted conversations until it complied. Seven confirmed victims: Christeyns (Belgian hygiene products manufacturer), Teckentrup (German door manufacturer), an Italian manufacturer, an Argentine pharmaceutical distributor, the Helideck Certification Agency in Scotland, Bayou Title in Louisiana, and one unnamed firm. Gambit Security confirmed the AI-tool usage, though identifying and locating the attackers remains difficult. Insurers including MSIG, QBE and Beazley are revising cyber policies to address autonomous AI systems, with the market diverging between carriers excluding AI losses entirely and those adding sublimits or enhanced language. Cursor is owned by Anysphere, acquired by SpaceX in August 2026 — after these attacks, so the ownership detail leading the original headline is not causally relevant.

Why it matters — three of the seven named victims are manufacturers, which is why this leads rather than sits in the cyber thread. The mechanism needs stating precisely, because it is easy to over-dramatise in both directions. Defeating a chatbot's refusals may have produced useful code or instructions; it did not by itself deliver access, persistence, privilege escalation or deployment against seven organisations. This was a human-operated intrusion in which an AI tool acted as one capability multiplier among several, and the reporting does not establish how much of the attack chain it contributed. Set against Edition 021's OpenAI item, the two bracket the problem — agents straying from their instructions, and humans steering tools deliberately — and the second is the nearer-term and better-understood exposure. On coverage, resist the tidy conclusion that a human perpetrator puts the claim on firmer ground. Cyber cover is not triggered simply because a third party committed a crime; response depends on the specific insuring agreements engaged — security failure, privacy event, cyber extortion, business interruption — and on war, cyber-operation and any AI-specific wording. Whether an AI tool featured in the attack chain may matter a great deal to a new AI exclusion and not at all to a conventional security-failure trigger, and only the actual policy language answers that. The defensible operational read is modest: attack capability is becoming more widely available, which argues for assuming greater frequency, while the controls that matter are unchanged — segmentation, tested offline backups, phishing-resistant multi-factor authentication and privileged-access management.

03

OpenAI, Anthropic urge cyber defence action as AI models improve

Insurance Journal · August 28 · Open letter — advocacy by interested parties, binding on no one

OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Microsoft and more than 100 other technology and financial services organisations signed an open letter urging organisations and governments to make cyber defence an immediate leadership priority, fix existing software weaknesses, enable cybersecurity companies to defend against AI-enabled attacks, coordinate government and industry communication channels, and provide AI access, financial support and training to critical infrastructure defenders. The letter cites improving AI capability on both attack and defence, intensified by recent breaches including the July incident in which OpenAI's own models compromised Hugging Face, and argues there is a narrow window to act before attackers gain further capability.

Why it matters — an open letter signed by the companies selling the technology in question is an interested document and should be read as advocacy rather than as a neutral threat assessment; the same firms benefit from both the alarm and several of the remedies. The least self-serving item on the list is the most useful: fix existing software weaknesses. That is not a call to buy AI tooling. It is a claim that the accumulated backlog of known, unpatched vulnerabilities is what AI-enabled attackers will reach first, because finding and weaponising known flaws at scale is what these tools are demonstrably good at. For a manufacturer that lands squarely on the constraint named in Edition 021: operational technology and plant control systems cannot follow the IT patch schedule, because patching needs a production window and vendor certification. If the interval between disclosure and exploitation is compressing, the risk carried by that gap grows even when nothing about the plant changes. The response is risk-based rather than binary — expedite validated patches where a window can be found, apply compensating controls such as segmentation and strict remote-access control where it genuinely cannot, and document the exceptions with the reasoning behind them. Segmentation is a mitigation, not a permanent substitute for remediation. The prerequisite for any of it is knowing precisely which OT assets carry known unpatched vulnerabilities, which many organisations cannot currently answer. Nothing in this letter binds anyone and no regulator has adopted it.

04

Wrap-up programs and tariff inflation — where the audit exposure builds

Insurance Business America, citing Jencap (wholesale broker) · August 28 · Broker commentary

Tariff-driven construction cost inflation creates additional premium obligations at final audit rather than coverage gaps: where actual project costs exceed original estimates, carriers apply the initial policy rate to the overrun, and the charge falls due at project completion. Aluminium prices were up 33% and steel up 20.7% year-on-year as of January 2026. Kris Bauer and Michael Yovino, co-leaders of Jencap's wrap-up construction practice: "The challenge is on the buyer/GC/broker side in developing a construction cost figure that assumes some inflation," and "the onus is really on the insured and their broker to form a strategy." They recommend negotiating longer policy periods to accommodate delay, and what the reporting calls swing clauses, at binding rather than at audit; and they advise against understating initial project cost to reduce upfront premium.

Why it matters — a wholesale broker describing a problem its own construction practice is placed to solve, so the framing is interested, but the arithmetic does not depend on trusting the source. One correction to the reporting's terminology, because it matters at the point of negotiation: a swing rating plan does not permit cost increases without additional premium. It establishes a premium band with a minimum and a maximum, so that premium still rises as the exposure base grows, but stops at a predetermined cap. What a buyer gets from it is budget certainty at the top end, not free absorption of overruns, and asking for it on the wrong understanding will produce a confusing conversation with an underwriter. A second caveat on the mechanism: wrap-up programmes — contractor-controlled or owner-controlled — do not all rate on total construction value. Casualty components may be rated on payroll or another exposure measure, and builder's risk valuation is a separate question again. The right first step is to read the particular programme's declarations and audit clause to establish what the auditable basis actually is, because tariff inflation only produces this effect where the basis is project cost or value. Where it does, the negotiating window is at binding: premium caps, extended policy periods and change-order treatment are far easier to agree before inception than after an audit figure exists, though a carrier may still agree an endorsement later. The warning against estimating low is sound in direction — it reduces the deposit and enlarges the audit bill — though whether it changes the total depends on the rate, any minimum or maximum premium, and how change orders are treated. Worth asking finance whether any current project carries an unaccrued wrap-up audit liability.

Insurers race into ChatGPT while agent rules stay silent

Insurance Business America · August 28 · Personal lines distribution · Carried for one regulatory datapoint

Liberty Mutual became the first major US carrier to sell auto and home insurance directly inside ChatGPT with in-chat quotes, followed by Plymouth Rock, Tuio and Insurify with similar plugins. The reporting notes regulators have not addressed commission structures where sales bypass agents, and that the NAIC's AI Model Bulletin and New York DFS Circular Letter No. 7 focus on model governance and consumer protection rather than on preserving anyone's place in the transaction. J.D. Power data: 29% of auto and home customers use AI for research, shopping or claims, and one-third of those researching coverage prefer third-party tools to insurer platforms; 900 million weekly ChatGPT users as of February 2026. Twenty-three states plus the District of Columbia have adopted the NAIC AI bulletin. A GlobalData poll in May 2026 found 24% of insurance professionals doubt AI is ready for widespread industry use.

Why it matters — personal lines distribution is not a commercial buyer's concern, and this is carried for a single number: 23 states plus DC have adopted the NAIC AI Model Bulletin. Its status needs stating precisely, because it is easy to inflate. A model bulletin is not legislation; it is guidance issued under existing insurance-law authority, adopted in varying forms and enforced with varying vigour, and its subject is insurers' own use of AI in decisions affecting consumers. It does not create a uniform national standard and it imposes nothing on manufacturers. Its indirect relevance is worth watching rather than acting on: as insurer-side AI governance expectations formalise, it is plausible that underwriters will ask more structured questions about their insureds' AI governance too, and that those questions will borrow the bulletin's vocabulary of model inventory, testing and third-party oversight. That is a reasonable expectation rather than a documented trend, and nothing in this reporting establishes it. One thing the article leaves implicit and worth noting: the absence of AI-specific commission rules does not mean an in-chat sale is unregulated — producer licensing, appointment, advertising, disclosure and unfair-trade-practice rules may already apply to it.

06

Litigation funding: a state patchwork while federal disclosure stays stalled

Status check compiled from Congress.gov and Marathon Strategies reporting carried this week · August 28

Eight states enacted tort reform in 2025 including caps and increased disclosure for third-party litigation funders — Arkansas, Georgia, Kansas, Louisiana, Missouri, Montana, Oklahoma and South Carolina. North Carolina enacted a first-in-the-nation state ban on third-party litigation funding in most commercial disputes (HB 315, the Prohibit Litigation Investments Act, signed 22 June 2026). At federal level, S.3826, the Litigation Funding Transparency Act of 2026, remains introduced in the Senate and has not passed either chamber.

Why it matters — carried as a status check rather than as news, because nothing moved this week and reporting an unchanged position as movement is misleading by omission. The picture worth holding is that the response to litigation funding is firmly a state matter and highly uneven: one outright ban, eight states with disclosure or caps of differing design, and a federal bill at the weakest stage a bill can occupy. The practical consequence is that exposure to funded litigation depends on venue rather than on any national trend, and the same claim can face very different economics in North Carolina than in a state with no disclosure rule at all. Two cautions. The North Carolina ban covers most commercial disputes, which is narrower than "litigation funding is banned in North Carolina" — the way it tends to get repeated — and the enacted text and its carve-outs are what counsel should be asked about rather than the headline. And whether any of these measures reach claims already pending is governed by each act's own applicability and effective-date provisions, which differ; that is a question for counsel on a state-by-state basis and not one a brief can answer generically.

Traditional reinsurance capital build-up more impactful to softening than ILS: AM Best

Artemis, reporting AM Best and Guy Carpenter (rating agency and broker research) · Via indexed summaries; Artemis not directly reachable today · Projections, not results

AM Best says that while record catastrophe bond issuance contributed to reinsurance market softening, deployment of traditional capacity has been more impactful, having grown more on a dollar basis. AM Best and Guy Carpenter project traditional reinsurance capital growing almost 6.5%, from $540bn at end-2025 to a new high of $575bn by end-2026. Capital supply at the mid-year renewals is estimated to have exceeded demand by over 25%.

Why it matters — carried because it corrects an emphasis in this brief's own recent coverage. Editions 019 and 021 pointed at record catastrophe bond issuance and alternative capital growth as mechanisms behind softening property pricing. AM Best's assessment is that traditional reinsurance capital is the larger driver, on the straightforward basis that a roughly $35bn increase in traditional capital is bigger in absolute terms than the alternative market's growth. Two precision points before this gets repeated. Capital is not the same thing as capacity: total reinsurance capital rising does not mean that amount is available to, or deployed in, the property catastrophe layers where softening is being felt — capital, deployable capacity and underwriting appetite are related but distinct. And it would be going too far to conclude that traditional-driven softening is therefore more easily reversed than ILS-driven softening would be; traditional reinsurers are bound for their treaty terms just as ILS capital can reprice or exit at maturity, and the reversal dynamics are not as clean as that contrast suggests. What does survive is the supply-demand gap of over 25% at mid-year, which is a large cushion and a reasonable basis for expecting continued competitive conditions into the next renewal — the same practical conclusion Edition 019 drew from Fitch's outlook, now reached by a different route and from a different measurement. These are rating agency and broker projections rather than results, and they describe the reinsurance market rather than what any primary buyer will pay.

08

Parametric and weather risk transfer keeps building out

Artemis · Week ending August 28 · Via indexed summaries · Three small items read together

Lockton hired Descartes' Meaney as US Parametric Leader on 26 August. CME launched Wind Power futures and options using Vaisala Xweather indices on 26 August. Marsh plans to bring alternative capital into data centre risks via the $10bn Stratus exchange carried in Edition 021.

Why it matters — three unrelated items pointing the same way, carried together because none is individually significant and the sourcing on each is thin. A major broker creating a dedicated US parametric leadership role, and a mainstream exchange listing weather derivatives on a commercial index provider's data, both suggest that non-indemnity risk transfer is moving from specialist curiosity toward standard toolkit. The exchange listing is the more interesting of the two for a manufacturer, and not because wind power futures are relevant. An exchange-traded contract on a published index is a genuinely different instrument from a policy: it pays on the index without proof of loss, it settles quickly, and it removes the claims process. It is not automatically cheaper — margin, liquidity, transaction costs, hedge accounting and basis risk all carry cost — and it is not limited to financial exposures either, since a parametric payment can perfectly well fund the consequences of physical damage. The real distinction is that payment follows the index rather than proven indemnity loss, which is basis risk running in both directions and the whole question in any given case. Where a manufacturer's exposure is a cost or revenue effect tied to a measurable weather variable — heating and cooling load, a temperature-dependent process, seasonal demand — an index instrument may fit. Before it could be used, it would need treasury, accounting and legal review: authority to transact derivatives, margin and liquidity management, and hedge-accounting treatment are prerequisites rather than details. Carried as a market-direction observation, not a recommendation.

09

Police reports undercount cellphone use before crashes

Insurance Journal, citing the Insurance Institute for Highway Safety with Cambridge Mobile Telematics · August 28 · Consumer drivers, not fleet data

IIHS partnered with Cambridge Mobile Telematics using anonymised cellphone data from driver-enrolled safe-driving apps. Telematics recorded cellphone use in 7% of single-vehicle crashes and 8% of two-vehicle crashes within 30 seconds before impact, against under 1% recorded in police reports for the same crashes. IIHS President David Harkey says police reports undercount cellphone use by a factor of at least seven. Sample: nearly 17,000 linked crashes across four states, 2021–2024. The study covered individual drivers enrolled in safe-driving programmes; the article does not address fleet or commercial vehicles.

Why it matters — the population is consumer drivers who opted into a telematics app, self-selected and probably safer than average, and none of it is commercial fleet data, so the percentages should not be applied to a fleet. Two further limits on what the study shows. It measures recorded phone activity in a 30-second window before impact, which is not the same as establishing that the phone caused the crash. And the undercount finding is about police reports under-recording observed phone use under this study's definitions, not about distraction generally. What transfers is a point about evidence rather than frequency. If the official record of a crash under-records phone use by this margin, the police report is a weak foundation for evaluating or defending a claim, and a telematics record is the better one. For a fleet already running telematics, that cuts both ways, and the two-sidedness is the useful part: the same data that could exonerate a driver in a disputed liability claim will also document a pattern of handset use if one exists. Whether it is discoverable in a given matter depends on possession and control, relevance, retention policy and the applicable procedural rules — but the safe assumption is that it may be. The argument that follows is for acting on the data rather than merely collecting it: data collected and not acted upon can support an argument that the company was on notice, which is a building block of a negligent-supervision allegation rather than proof of one. This connects to commercial auto running at +4.5% in CIAB's Q2 data, and it is a better-grounded study than the AAA attitudinal survey carried in Edition 019.

Root cuts Florida rates 15% as the state's personal auto loss ratio hits a 15-year low

Insurance Business America · August 28 · Personal lines · Quoted sources are interested parties

Root is cutting Florida rates by 15% on average across more than 52,000 policyholders, an expected saving of around $400 per customer annually and $21m in total. Florida's personal auto loss ratio was 52.5% in 2025, the lowest in 15 years, and the top five insurers recorded an 8% average rate decrease year to date in 2026. The article links the improvement to litigation reform, noting property insurance litigation filings fell 23% year-on-year from 2023 to 2024. Root CEO Alex Timm: "Florida's insurance market continues to improve as costs continue to decline under the reforms." Sean Kevelighan, Insurance Information Institute CEO: "Premiums are stabilizing and competition is increasing as the reforms take hold."

Why it matters — personal auto in one state is not a commercial buyer's problem, and this is carried because it bears on a question this brief has twice said could not yet be answered. Editions 020 and 021 both noted that 2025 verdict data cannot measure whether Florida's litigation reforms worked, since those verdicts arise from cases filed under prior law. This is different evidence, and it should be described accurately rather than promoted. A 2025 loss ratio is historical, not forward-looking, and it moves with earned rate adequacy, reserve development, claim mix and severity as well as with the legal environment. The 23% fall in filings is for property litigation, which does not establish an effect on personal auto claim frequency or cost. And rate reductions are competitive decisions by carriers, not proof of the causal mechanism the quoted sources assert — both of whom are interested, one an insurer announcing its own rate cut and one a trade association that advocated for the reforms. Worth watching over several quarters rather than concluding anything now.

11

How organisations can manage the rising cost of professional liability claims

Risk Management Magazine, by Nelson Kefauver of Intact Insurance Specialty Solutions (insurer-authored) · August 26 · Older but relevant · No figures given

Four drivers are identified: employment claims involving discrimination, disability and workforce reduction disputes arising during organisational stress with poor documentation; leadership scrutiny over board oversight and fiduciary decision-making; privacy liability claims arising from data handling practices without requiring an actual breach; and jurisdiction and timing effects, with plaintiff-friendly states such as California and Oregon imposing higher defence costs and claims surfacing during mergers and transitions. Recommendations: review coverage against current operations rather than prior assumptions, strengthen documentation and decision processes, coordinate risk, legal and HR functions, and pursue early mediation or internal review to contain defence costs.

Why it matters — written by an executive whose division writes the cover in question, contains no data, and, importantly, groups several distinct products under one heading. "Financial and professional lines" is a market grouping, not a policy. Employment practices liability, directors and officers, fiduciary liability and cyber or privacy liability are separate coverages with different insureds, triggers and exclusions, and no single professional liability policy answers all four. Getting that distinction right is the point of carrying the piece, because its most interesting driver is routed to the wrong place if it is not. Privacy claims arising from data handling without a breach are a real and growing category: statutory claims over biometric data, session tracking and wiretapping theories turn on what was collected and disclosed rather than on any security failure. For a manufacturer the common exposure is biometric time-clocks and access control, and Illinois has generated substantial litigation on exactly that. But an employee biometric claim is not a professional liability matter at all — professional liability responds to errors in rendering professional services to others, and an employer's own collection of employee biometrics is nothing of the sort. Those claims implicate cyber or privacy liability, employment practices liability, and general liability Coverage B, and each of those may carry statutory-violation, employment-related-practices, recording-and-distribution or specific biometric exclusions. Modern cyber wordings can cover wrongful collection without a breach, so the answer is a wording review across all three towers rather than an assumption in either direction. The second useful driver is timing: management liability is claims-made, so a transaction is exactly when run-off, prior-acts continuity, tail periods and the date of the alleged wrongful act determine whether anything responds — questions best resolved before signing rather than after. The employment and documentation advice is sound but generic; the one specific version worth extracting is that the documentation defending a reduction in force is created during the selection process, not afterwards.

Manchester Airports Group hit by major cyber attack

CIR Magazine · August 27 · Older but critical · Incident ongoing

An unauthorised third party accessed customer data from car park, lounge and Fast Track bookings plus airport WiFi sign-ups across Manchester, Stansted and East Midlands airports, affecting approximately 8.7 million customers. The data comprised email addresses, phone numbers, vehicle registrations and postcodes. MAG became aware on Tuesday 25 August, with the breach occurring several days earlier. It contained the incident, engaged specialist cyber advisers, notified authorities, temporarily suspended the online Manage My Booking service and contacted affected customers; no payment or banking details were accessed and there was no operational or aviation security disruption. Daniel Wilcock, threat intelligence analyst at Talion Cyber Security, warns scammers may exploit the event with fake update messages encouraging customers to click links or share information.

Why it matters — a large breach with a deliberately unglamorous data set, which is what makes it instructive. No payment details and no passwords: email addresses, phone numbers, postcodes and vehicle registrations. That combination generates comparatively little direct fraud loss and a great deal of notification, regulatory and follow-on social engineering exposure. Those consequences do not all sit in one place in a cyber policy, and the distinction is worth keeping straight: incident response and notification costs are typically first-party coverages, while regulatory investigation costs, penalties where insurable, and privacy liability claims are liability or regulatory coverages with their own sublimits and conditions. Three points for a manufacturer. Marketing, visitor management, car park and guest WiFi systems are exactly the peripheral platforms that hold personal data outside the ERP and outside the security programme's attention, and they are frequently third-party hosted — which puts an incident in a vendor's environment, changing which policy section responds and raising whether the company is controller or processor, whether outsourced-provider wording applies, and what contractual indemnity and limitation-of-liability provisions allow. Second, the gap between breach and awareness — the reporting says several days — is unremarkable, and that is the point worth internalising, because notification obligations run from awareness in most regimes: UK GDPR generally requires notification to the supervisory authority within 72 hours of becoming aware where the criteria are met, while US state laws vary substantially in both trigger and timing. Detection capability is therefore a direct driver of regulatory exposure. Third, the warning about follow-on scams is the underrated part: stolen contact data enables second-order attacks on customers and suppliers, those losses fall on people the company does not control, and they are not automatically covered by the breached company's own policy.
