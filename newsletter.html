CFO Brief — three takeaways, written to be read aloud
  1. Market — two Q2 datasets landed today and they point in opposite directions, which is itself the finding: the softening is real but narrow, not a market-wide turn. The Council of Insurance Agents & Brokers, a broker trade association surveying its own members, reports average premiums across all accounts down 2%, large accounts down 3.7%, commercial property down 6.3%, cyber and workers' compensation each down 3.2%, with 75% of respondents seeing more property capacity. Umbrella rose 5.3% — a 35th consecutive quarterly increase — and commercial auto 4.5%. But the Ivans Index, which measures renewal rate change on business flowing through agency management systems and skews to small and mid-market accounts, still shows commercial property rising 6.40%, merely slower than Q1's 6.83%. Both can be true, and the most likely reconciliation is account size and method — though neither publisher says so, so treat that as inference rather than fact. Three cautions before this is repeated to anyone. Where a property schedule sits between those two buckets determines whether there is relief at all. Premium is not rate: changes in values, limits and structure move premium independently. And a softening market delivers price relief bundled with narrower wordings, higher deductibles and new sublimits at least as often as not, so any improvement has to be read against terms, not just the number. Workers' compensation is soft on both measures, which is the one unambiguous good news.
  2. Liability — a unanimous Supreme Court ruling has stripped freight brokers of a preemption defence, and the practical question it raises is who picks the carriers. In Montgomery v. Caribe Transport II the Court unanimously rejected freight brokers' federal preemption defence, allowing state-law negligent-hiring claims against brokers to proceed. Precision matters here: preemption is not immunity, and permitting a claim to proceed decides nothing about negligence or damages in any case. The reporting cites a $604m jury verdict recommendation against C.H. Robinson from a fatal 2021 collision — a recommendation, not an entered judgment — brokers sharply contracting carrier networks, and double-digit increases in transportation insurance premiums. This creates no new direct liability for a manufacturer that ships goods, and it should not be presented as if it does. The economic effect is that brokered freight gets more expensive and the usable carrier pool narrows, though whether that cost lands on brokers, carriers, shippers or consumers is genuinely unsettled. The durable point is the negligent-selection theory itself: it turns on who chose the motor carrier and on what basis. Where a shipper selects carriers itself, or a contract puts that choice on it, the questions are what vetting standard applies, whether it is documented, and what the contracts say about indemnity, additional-insured status and evidence of insurance.
  3. Supply chain — exposure mapping probably stops one tier too early, and reported tariff relief does not undo the reroutes already made. Deloitte's Center for Integrated Research reports that about 33% of executives it surveyed in 2025 had a natural disaster or severe weather event affect their business, that around one-fifth of companies have a climate adaptation plan and roughly half assess supplier risk exposure at all, and that such analysis commonly stops at tier-1 suppliers — citing a 2021 Texas semiconductor shutdown that cost one company an estimated $100m. Separately, US–Canada tariff reductions are reported as potential rather than confirmed — vehicles from 25% to 15% and steel and aluminium from 50% to 25% — with no effective date established in the reporting, so no claims-cost conclusion should assume they take effect. Nationwide's point is the durable one: lower tariffs will not reverse supply-chain decisions already taken, even if some reroutes eventually reverse on their own. This runs straight into yesterday's correction on dependent-property cover. Contingent business interruption generally requires physical loss or damage at a qualifying dependent property and, depending on wording, may be limited to scheduled suppliers or extend on a sublimited blanket basis. So a tier-2 dependency nobody has mapped may or may not be covered — and that is exactly the point: nobody currently knows. Mapping the tiers and reading the dependent-property wording are one project, and neither answers the question alone.

01

CIAB survey: overall soft market conditions continue in Q2

Insurance Journal, citing the Council of Insurance Agents & Brokers (broker trade association) · August 20 · Survey research

Average premiums across all accounts fell 2% in the second quarter of 2026, with large accounts down 3.7%, medium accounts down 1.9% and small accounts down 0.5%. By line, commercial property fell 6.3%, cyber 3.2% and workers' compensation 3.2%, while umbrella rose 5.3% — its 35th consecutive quarterly increase — and commercial auto rose 4.5%. Seventy-five per cent of respondents noted an increase in property capacity, which the survey identifies as exerting downward pressure on premiums as carriers competed for clients.

Why it matters — this is a trade association surveying its own broker members, so it measures what brokers report achieving rather than an audited index, and the large-account band is the most volatile of the three because a handful of substantial placements can move the average. One technical caution belongs on it: the survey measures premium, not rate. Premium moves when insured values, limits, payroll, sales or programme structure change, entirely independently of whether the underwriter's rate went up or down, so a reported decrease is not by itself evidence of rate relief on a comparable basis. Even discounted for both of those, the split is the useful part and it is the same split that shows up in the Ivans data below: property and cyber are easing on capacity while umbrella and commercial auto continue to rise on severity. For a manufacturer that argues for approaching the two halves of the programme quite differently this year — testing the property placement properly, and defending the casualty tower. What the survey does not describe at all is terms, and a softening market habitually gives back on wording, deductibles and sublimits some of what it concedes on price.

02

Commercial insurance rate increases cool across most lines in second quarter

Risk & Insurance, citing the Ivans Index (vendor data) · August 19 · 1 day old

Average premium renewal rate change fell quarter over quarter for every major commercial line except workers' compensation. Umbrella came in at 7.96% against 9.36% in the first quarter, commercial auto at 4.93% against 5.28%, businessowners policies at 6.16% against 6.74%, general liability at 5.44% against 6.85%, commercial property at 6.40% against 6.83%, and workers' compensation at −1.37% against −1.73%.

Why it matters — this dataset and the CIAB survey above appear to contradict one another, and the contradiction is worth putting in front of the CFO rather than smoothing over. Ivans shows every line except workers' compensation still rising, merely more slowly; CIAB shows most lines falling outright. The likeliest explanation lies in what each one measures. Ivans records renewal rate change on policies flowing through agency management systems, which weights heavily toward small and mid-market business, while CIAB surveys brokers about the accounts they place, which skews considerably larger. Both can therefore be accurate descriptions of different populations. Neither publisher offers that reconciliation, however, so it should be carried as inference rather than as established fact. The practical consequence is specific and worth stating plainly: where our property schedule sits between those two populations determines which of the two numbers actually describes our renewal. A headline of "commercial property is down 6.3%" should not travel into a budget conversation without that caveat attached, because if our placement behaves like mid-market business the relevant figure is an increase of 6.40%, not a decrease.

US–Canada tariff cuts could ease auto and property claims costs

Insurance Business America, citing Nationwide · August 20 · Carrier commentary · reported as prospective, not confirmed

On the reporting, Canadian vehicle tariffs may fall from 25% to 15% and steel and aluminium duties from 50% to 25%. The article cites a spring 2025 estimate of $30–61bn in potential added personal auto claim costs over a twelve-month period, notes that personal auto represents nearly a third of US property and casualty premium volume, and reports US reconstruction costs rising 3.6% between April 2025 and April 2026, down from 5.2% in the preceding period. It further cites 49% of businesses renegotiating or diversifying supply chains, with only 3% describing their chains as very resilient. Nationwide's Kristina Talkowski returns to the lead-time point, noting that supply-chain shifts change material lead times.

Why it matters — status before substance, because the status is doing a lot of work here. The reporting describes these reductions as prospective rather than in force, and gives no effective date, covered-goods list or origin rules. Nothing should be budgeted on them. Even if they do take effect, a lower headline tariff percentage does not convert directly into lower claims costs: scope, exemptions, exchange rates, inventory already purchased at the old rate and movements in the underlying commodity price all sit between the two. The $30–61bn figure deserves the same care — it is a projection made in spring 2025 about what tariffs might add to personal auto claims, not a measured outcome, and personal auto is not our exposure in any event. It is carried purely as an indication of the scale at which tariff policy touches insurance loss costs. The durable point is the one made in yesterday's edition and it has not changed: tariff relief does not un-reroute a supply chain. Suppliers changed during the dispute may or may not revert, and the insurance consequence of the change sits in the dependent-property schedule rather than anywhere in the tariff line.

Climate risk analysis rarely reaches beyond tier 1 suppliers

Risk & Insurance, citing the Deloitte Center for Integrated Research (consultancy research), with supplementary data from S&P Global and CDP · August 20

Around 33% of global executives surveyed by Deloitte in 2025 reported that natural disasters and severe weather had affected their business. Only about one-fifth of companies have a climate adaptation action plan, and roughly half assess supplier risk exposure. The research observes that physical climate risks frequently originate well outside a company's direct operations yet still cause cascading disruption through interconnected value chains, and cites a 2021 Texas semiconductor plant shutdown estimated to have caused $100m of disruption to a single company. It proposes a five-part resilience framework covering risk mapping, operational adjustment, financial planning, governance and regulatory adaptation.

Why it matters — this is consultancy research promoting a framework the firm sells, so the framework itself is worth rather less than the finding underneath it. One methodological caution: the percentages shift between executives surveyed and companies generally, so they are not all measuring the same base and should not be stacked together as though they were. What survives that discount is the core observation, and it is a good one — supplier climate analysis commonly stops at tier 1, while the dependencies capable of halting a production line frequently sit further down, in a sole-source supplier's own sole-source input. That is the exposure counterpart to the coverage point this brief made yesterday. Contingent business interruption generally requires physical loss or damage at a qualifying dependent property, and whether an unmapped tier-2 dependency would be covered at all depends on whether the wording operates on a scheduled basis, a blanket basis or an unnamed-supplier basis, each with different sublimits. It would be wrong to assert that such a supplier is uncovered; the accurate and more uncomfortable statement is that most buyers do not currently know, because nobody has mapped the tier and nobody has read the wording against it. Those two tasks are one project and neither answers the question on its own.

A landmark Supreme Court ruling is upending how America moves its goods

Insurance Journal / Bloomberg · August 20 · Supreme Court ruling

In Montgomery v. Caribe Transport II the Supreme Court unanimously rejected freight brokers' federal preemption defence, allowing state-law negligent-hiring suits against brokers to proceed on motor-vehicle safety claims. The reporting cites a $604m jury verdict recommendation against C.H. Robinson arising from a Texas case involving a fatal 2021 Mississippi collision; freight brokers sharply contracting their carrier networks, with one broker reducing from 15,000–18,000 carriers to about 8,000; strong double-digit increases in transportation insurance premiums; and significant declines in the stock values of major brokers since the verdict was disclosed. A transportation attorney quoted in the piece observed that higher freight insurance costs "all go somewhere. Usually they end up on the consumer."

Why it matters — the most consequential item in today's edition, and also the one most easily over-read, so two precisions come first. Preemption is not immunity: the Court removed a federal defence that brokers had been raising, it did not create liability, and allowing claims to proceed determines nothing about negligence or damages in any individual case. And the $604m is described as a jury verdict recommendation rather than an entered judgment, with the post-trial and appellate posture unstated, so it indicates the scale of jury sentiment rather than anyone's settled exposure. With those in place, what actually reaches a manufacturer that ships goods is economic and contractual rather than legal. Brokered freight will cost more, and the pool of carriers brokers are willing to use is contracting — which tightens capacity precisely on the lanes that were cheapest, because the carriers being dropped are the marginal ones. Where that additional cost finally settles, as between brokers, carriers, shippers and consumers, is genuinely unsettled and the quoted prediction is one lawyer's view rather than a finding. The durable question is the negligent-selection theory itself, which turns on who chose the motor carrier and on what basis. Where we select carriers directly, or where a transportation contract or shipper-broker agreement places that responsibility on us, the internal questions are what carrier-vetting standard we apply, whether we document applying it, and what our contracts say about indemnity, additional-insured status and evidence of insurance.

Zuckerberg did not make child safety a priority at Meta, former engineer testifies

Insurance Journal / Reuters · August 20 · Trial testimony — allegation, not finding

Former Meta engineering director Arturo Bejar testified in a federal trial in Oakland, California, in a suit brought by a coalition of 29 states led by California, Colorado, Kentucky and New Jersey, alleging that Meta designed Facebook and Instagram to addict young users while illegally collecting children's data. Bejar, who worked at Meta from 2009 to 2015 and again from 2019 to 2021, testified that the company used metrics rewarding engagement over safety and characterised a break-reminder tool as designed to fail because users could easily ignore it. The trial is expected to last six weeks, Zuckerberg is expected to testify, and Meta has denied all allegations.

Why it matters — this is the testimony of a single witness in a trial that has not concluded. It is evidence offered in support of an allegation, not a finding, and nothing in it has been established. It is carried because it is the live test of the "addictive design" theory that this brief covered on 11 August, in which the wrongful act alleged is a deliberate design choice rather than a defect or an accident. One correction to how that was framed at the time, because it was too strong. An intentional design decision does not by itself defeat the occurrence requirement in a commercial general liability policy: an intended act can and frequently does cause unintended injury, and most jurisdictions analyse the question by reference to whether the injury was expected or intended rather than whether the conduct was deliberate. Whether cover responds therefore turns on the expected-or-intended exclusion, on how the specific claim is pleaded, and on the governing jurisdiction — and in practice technology errors and omissions, cyber, privacy and directors and officers wordings are considerably more likely to be in play on a claim of this shape than a CGL policy. The connection for a manufacturer runs to connected consumer products with engagement or retention features designed in. Worth watching the outcome; not worth acting on testimony.

Litigation funding — a federal rules amendment, not the bill, is the route to watch

Congress.gov for the bill; the Rule 26 proposal filed by the Institute for Legal Reform (tort-reform advocacy body) and Lawyers for Civil Justice · bill introduced February 2026 · proposal filed 10 March 2026

The Litigation Funding Transparency Act of 2026 (S.3826) remains introduced and pending in the Senate Judiciary Committee, where it has sat since February — no committee vote, no floor action, no enactment. Separately, the US Chamber Institute for Legal Reform and Lawyers for Civil Justice filed a joint proposal on 10 March asking that Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 26(a)(1)(A) be amended to require parties to disclose, at the outset of a case, the identity of any non-party funder with a financial interest in the litigation and the agreements that define that interest.

Why it matters — this is a correction to how this brief has been framing the issue for several weeks. The item has tracked S.3826 and reported no movement, which was accurate but materially incomplete: legislation is not the only route to a federal disclosure requirement, and arguably not the likeliest one. An amendment to the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure would achieve much of the same result and does not require Congress to pass anything affirmatively. What it does require is a great deal else, and the distinction matters for how this is reported. A proposal filed by two advocacy organisations is a request, not a pending amendment; the Advisory Committee on Civil Rules has to take it up at all, after which it runs through committee consideration, publication for public comment, the Judicial Conference, transmission by the Supreme Court and a congressional review window — a process routinely measured in years rather than months. Nothing has changed for any matter currently on foot, and nothing will for some time. The reason to track it is that a disclosure requirement, if one ever arrives, would alter settlement dynamics in funded litigation by revealing who stands behind a claim. Even that expectation deserves qualification: funding agreements may be produced under protective order or in redacted form, and knowing the identity of a funder does not tell a defendant what return that funder needs in order to settle.

Soft inland marine market opens door to higher limits as equipment values surge

Insurance Business America, citing Amwins Program Underwriters (wholesale broker and program underwriter) · August 20

Jason Baynard, senior vice president at Amwins Program Underwriters, describes an inland marine market currently favourable to buyers, with expanded carrier capacity and reduced pricing, noting that insurers are drawn to the class because "with first-party coverage, you know exactly what the loss is when that piece of equipment catches fire." The article identifies three trends: data centre construction driving rising equipment concentrations in temporary storage, with temporary storage limits potentially reaching $30–50m and warehouse legal liability cover coming into play; tariffs materially raising replacement cost, illustrated by an $8m crane purchase that incurred an additional $1.4m in tariff costs; and growth in loss-of-use cover, providing monthly payments when key equipment is unavailable.

Why it matters — a wholesale broker describing a market it sells into, so the enthusiasm is interested, but the timing observation underneath is checkable and genuinely useful. Inland marine is the line covering movable equipment and property in transit, which for a manufacturer means mobile plant, tooling moving between sites, contractors' equipment and property at temporary locations. Two things have moved at once: capacity has loosened, and tariffs have raised what it costs to replace the identical machine. That combination is the argument for revisiting values and limits now rather than waiting for renewal, because a schedule valued before the tariff round is understated against current replacement cost and an understated schedule is a coinsurance problem as well as a limits problem. One expectation to set: additional limit will ordinarily carry additional premium even in a competitive market, because accumulation and replacement values drive the rating — a soft market makes the increase easier to obtain, not free. Two technical notes on the specifics. Warehouse legal liability protects a bailee against liability for other people's property, so it is the relevant cover if we store customer goods, not a cover for our own equipment sitting in temporary storage. And loss-of-use is not a substitute for equipment breakdown insurance, which is the established, specialised cover for the failure of a single critical machine and may well already sit inside the property programme — the question to ask is how the two interact and where the gaps between them fall, not whether to buy one in place of the other.

Medicare's 2026 fee schedule shifts signal modest but uneven pressure on workers' comp costs

Risk & Insurance, citing NCCI (workers' compensation rating and data organisation) · August 19 · 1 day old

NCCI's analysis reports a 3.3% increase in the physician conversion factor, a 2.6% facility payment increase and a 2.0% update to the DMEPOS fee schedule. Physician costs represent roughly 40% of workers' compensation medical expenditure and facility costs a further 40%, while durable medical equipment ranges from 4% to 13% depending on the state. NCCI assesses these federal adjustments as "not expected to be a significant source of upward pressure on overall workers' compensation medical costs," noting that the impact varies substantially by state according to how closely each state's fee schedule mirrors CMS rates and which service categories it covers.

Why it matters — the direction needs stating carefully, because it is easy to invert. Higher fee schedule amounts mean providers are paid more for the same treatment, so this is a modest headwind on workers' compensation medical costs — upward pressure, not relief. NCCI's own conclusion is that the aggregate effect should not be significant, and that is the honest summary to carry: a mild cost pressure rather than a warning. NCCI is the industry's workers' compensation rating and data organisation, which makes it technically authoritative on this subject without making it disinterested. The physician conversion factor rising 3.3% after five years of decline is the genuinely notable element, since physician costs are the largest single component of the medical spend. Where this becomes practical for a multi-state manufacturer is the state variation, which NCCI emphasises: states that peg their workers' compensation fee schedule closely to Medicare will pass the increase through, and states that set their own schedules will not, so the effect on our loss costs depends entirely on our state mix. A single question to the broker or third-party administrator about which of our states track CMS rates would settle it. Note finally that this concerns medical costs only and says nothing about indemnity costs or about claim frequency, which are the larger drivers of a workers' compensation result.
