Judge rejects First Brands' plan to pay down debts by pursuing lawsuits

Insurance Journal · Aug 25 · bankruptcy / supply chain
US Bankruptcy Judge Christopher Lopez rejected the Chapter 11 reorganisation plan of First Brands, an auto-parts manufacturer that filed in September with more than $9bn of liabilities against $14m of cash, and converted the case to Chapter 7 liquidation. The proposed litigation trust would have required roughly $1.9bn of lawsuit recoveries before creditors were made whole. During the bankruptcy the company sold its Horizon towing, Toledo Molding & Die and Walbro fuel-system lines for about $194m combined. A separate $286m claim alleges tariffs fraud, and founder Patrick James and his brother Edward face fraud charges. Why it matters: the transferable lesson is in the supply chain rather than the courtroom, and it runs in two directions that need separating because they have different insurance answers. Buying from a supplier in this position is a continuity exposure: converting to Chapter 7 materially reduces the prospect of sustained operation, since a trustee's mandate is to liquidate, and while a court can authorise temporary operation where that benefits the estate, it is not a going-concern outcome. Contingent business interruption under a property policy generally requires physical loss or damage from a covered cause at the dependent property; an insolvency causes none, so ordinary CBI does not respond to a supplier simply failing, and supply-chain or non-damage trade-disruption wording has to have been bought separately. CBI forms also differ on whether unnamed direct suppliers are covered at a sublimit or only scheduled locations respond, and most restrict sub-tier suppliers. Selling to a distressed customer is the opposite exposure — the receivable — and trade credit is the relevant product, stated correctly: the insured is the seller, the customer is an approved buyer under a credit limit the insurer sets, and those limits can be reduced or withdrawn prospectively as a buyer deteriorates. Receivables already overdue or in dispute at inception are generally excluded, so cover arranged after a customer is visibly distressed is arranged too late. On the D&O angle reported here, which is the founder's rather than a counterparty's: conduct exclusions vary materially in wording — some require a final non-appealable adjudication, some an adjudication in the underlying action, and some are broader — so indictment is not the dividing line, and Side A responds only to covered non-indemnifiable loss subject to every other term. The tariff-fraud claim is an allegation at this stage, not a finding.

The AI clause that may already be on your contractor client's policy

Insurance Business America · Aug 25 · coverage / AI
ISO published standard generative-AI exclusion forms in July 2025 and carriers began filing them a month later. The publication counts 4,078 state-level filing records across 49 states and the District of Columbia, of which 2,369 were in force as of 31 July 2026. Three versions are described: CG 40 47, the broadest, reaching bodily injury, property damage, personal and advertising injury and completed operations; a narrower advertising-injury-only version; and one aimed at completed operations, reported as CG 35 08. The forms are appearing in commercial general liability, umbrella and excess, commercial package and businessowners programmes, and the article reports carriers extending comparable exclusions to errors-and-omissions business. Matt Levin, research head at an insurance research firm, notes that a business might use AI for a single estimate, takeoff or design decision and do the rest of the job without it, and the exclusion could still reach a later claim. Contractor AI adoption is cited as rising from 17% in 2025 to 38% in 2026. Why it matters: the article is written for brokers advising contractors, but the ISO filing data behind it applies to commercial general liability generally, which is why it belongs here. The framing to resist is "we don't use AI, so this doesn't apply" — what matters is whether the endorsement's causal wording reaches the work that produced the loss, not whether the company has an AI strategy. Three qualifications keep that from being overstated. First, these are generative-AI exclusions, and their reach depends on how each form defines the term: conventional machine learning, machine vision inspection and statistical predictive-maintenance tools are not automatically caught, though a vendor tool that acquires a generative feature by update is exactly the ambiguous case worth asking about. Second, filing counts measure availability, not attachment, and they say nothing about how a carrier deploys a form in practice — a broadly filed exclusion may be applied narrowly or only to certain classes. Third, no court has construed the wording. The article reports no admitted standalone product providing a complete substitute, but "AI exposure" is not a single coverage part: portions may sit in cyber, technology E&O, media or manuscript endorsements, and surplus-lines markets may offer more than the admitted market does. The concrete step is to obtain the schedule of forms with edition dates for GL, umbrella, E&O and D&O, and to ask what buy-back or affirmative grant was available.

US severe convective storm industry losses exceed $35bn in 2026 so far

Artemis, reporting Gallagher Re (reinsurance broker) · August 2026 — Artemis returned 403 on direct fetch today, so this is drawn from indexed summaries of its reporting and no exact publication day is claimed · catastrophe
Gallagher Re puts year-to-date US insured losses from severe convective storm above $35bn, up from roughly $22bn estimated in mid-June. The acceleration came from an active stretch across the Plains, Midwest and Mid-Atlantic between 9 and 12 August, including a high-end derecho on 11 August that caused significant damage across the Chicago metropolitan area and other population centres in Indiana, Ohio and Kentucky. Gallagher Re notes this is at least the fourth consecutive year in which US SCS losses have crossed $35bn nominally, against a 2021–2025 average of about $50bn and a 2016–2025 average of about $40bn in today's dollars. Illinois has recorded more than 200 confirmed tornadoes year to date, a state record, beating 142 in 2024. Why it matters: convective storm is the peril most likely to reach a Midwestern manufacturing footprint and the one most likely to be under-managed, because no single event is large enough to force attention. Hail and straight-line wind concentrate damage on roofs, rooftop HVAC and process equipment, skylights and yard stock — exactly the exposures where valuation and margin clauses, cosmetic-damage limitations, actual-cash-value roof endorsements and separate wind/hail percentage deductibles decide the recovery. Read the $35bn carefully: it is a broker's running estimate of industry insured loss, revised as claims develop, not a settled figure, and it is nominal, so comparing it to the inflation-adjusted averages is not like for like — on that basis the year is running below both. The specific items worth checking on a manufacturer's own programme are how the percentage deductible is applied (to total insured value at a location, or only to damaged property), whether it applies per occurrence or per location, roof age and valuation basis, and ordinance-or-law and debris-removal sublimits, which is where convective-storm recoveries most often fall short.

Reno wildfire grows to 15,000 acres as Nevada's new wildfire exclusion law faces its first real test

Insurance Business America · Aug 25 · catastrophe / regulation
A wildfire near Reno had reached roughly 15,000 acres and was uncontained as of Monday, with about 90,000 people under evacuation notices; the Reno Fire Department warned the blaze could stay active for another 48 hours. The article frames this as the first major Reno-area fire since Nevada's Assembly Bill 376 took effect on 1 January 2026. AB 376, passed unanimously in June 2025, permits insurers to remove wildfire coverage from standard homeowners policies entirely — either offering it as a separate product or with no replacement — under a four-year regulatory sandbox. Nevada has no FAIR Plan, so there is no state insurer of last resort for residents unable to obtain separate wildfire cover. No insured loss estimate was given. Why it matters: the direct exposure is personal lines, not commercial, the fire is active so any figure quoted this week will move, and whether this actually tests AB 376 depends on whether affected policies carry the permitted exclusion and whether denials or availability problems follow. It may prove the first practical test; it is not one yet. The reason it belongs in a commercial brief is precedent: a US state has authorised carriers to strip a named peril out of a standard policy form with no residual-market backstop behind it, and the structure is time-limited and reversible but worth watching in case it is copied. Two limits: AB 376 as described reaches homeowners policies, so it does not by its terms touch commercial property, and the reporting does not set out the statute's eligibility, notice, disclosure or mitigation conditions, which would matter before concluding insurers can simply remove the peril. For any facility, workforce or supplier concentration in northern Nevada the near-term questions are the ordinary ones, and the trigger detail matters more than the headline. Civil authority coverage ordinarily requires an order that prohibits or impairs access, arising from covered physical damage to other property, within a stated distance and subject to a waiting period and a time limit; an evacuation advisory near an active fire is not by itself enough. Ingress/egress wording varies on whether an order or physical damage is required at all. Smoke damage, utility interruption and workforce disruption are each separate questions from direct fire damage.

NAIC extends private-rating deadlines as it recovers from June ShinyHunters breach

Insurance Business America · Aug 25 · cyber / regulation
The NAIC's Securities Valuation Office received approval to extend private-rating filing deadlines by a period equal to the length of the outage that began on 17 June, implemented in the VISION filing application. The breach traces to a critical Oracle PeopleSoft vulnerability, CVE-2026-35273, rated 9.8 out of 10 and exploitable remotely without authentication, which affected more than 100 organisations globally. ShinyHunters claimed 3.1 terabytes across more than 105,000 files from NAIC systems, but the NAIC's investigation, with FBI involvement, concluded the access was more limited and that exposed data was confined to statutory financial reporting already public through state insurance department websites and to credit rating agency determinations — with no personally identifiable information, banking data, policyholder information or confidential investment portfolio detail accessed. Designations dependent on the VISION and STS systems remain in transition. Why it matters: the insurance-specific consequence is administrative and belongs to insurers' own investment and financial reporting teams, not to policyholders, and separating that from the headline is most of the value. For a manufacturer the story is a supply-chain-software case study, and the mechanism is the familiar one: a single unauthenticated remote-code-execution flaw in a widely deployed enterprise platform, exploited across more than a hundred organisations at once. PeopleSoft and its peers sit in plenty of manufacturing back offices. The practical checks are patch status against CVE-2026-35273 and whether the affected systems are internet-facing. On coverage, a vendor's breach is not automatically a cyber policy's problem, and the trigger is more demanding than "our supplier was hacked": dependent or contingent business interruption under a cyber policy ordinarily requires a covered security failure or system failure at the provider and a resulting interruption or degradation of the insured's own operations that exceeds the waiting period, with the wording's definition of a dependent provider deciding whether an unscheduled vendor counts at all. A vendor data breach with no operational impact generally triggers nothing. Note finally the gap between an attacker's claim of 3.1 terabytes and an investigation's finding of limited access: attacker volume claims are marketing, and the investigation's figure is the one to use.

Parametric insurance market projected to reach $7.64bn by 2031

GlobeNewswire — commercial market-research release; a research vendor's projection, not an industry statistic · Aug 14 · older, carried for scale
A commercially published market report projects the parametric insurance market reaching US$7.64bn by 2031, attributing growth to accelerating climate risk. The release is a marketing summary of a paid report; methodology, market definition and base year are not disclosed in the public summary. Why it matters: included for scale, with the caveat doing most of the work. Vendor market-sizing releases are promotional, and definitions vary so widely — whether structured parametric reinsurance, sovereign risk pools and catastrophe bonds are counted materially changes the total — that this should never be quoted to a board as an industry figure. What is worth taking is directional and corroborated elsewhere in recent editions: parametric structures keep appearing precisely where indemnity cover struggles, in cloud outage, non-damage business interruption, and perils with slow or contested loss adjustment. For a manufacturer the honest use case is narrow. Payment is determined by a contractual trigger rather than by adjusted loss, which is what makes it fast and what makes it useful for liquidity and for exposures indemnity wordings do not reach — but corporate parametric policies generally still require an insurable interest and often a proof or attestation of loss, both to preserve their character as insurance rather than a derivative and to avoid a windfall, so "pays regardless of loss" overstates it. Basis risk — the gap between trigger and actual loss — runs both ways and the adverse side is the buyer's, and although there is no conventional loss adjustment, wordings do commonly contain data-source, calculation-agent and dispute provisions worth reading. It supplements property cover; it does not replace it.

Trump's environmental rollbacks are being struck down by courts

Insurance Journal · Aug 24 · regulation / environmental
A review of litigation over the administration's EPA deregulatory actions finds the agency losing most of the significant cases. The DC Circuit ruled 3-0 in June to uphold stronger fine-particulate (soot) standards; a federal judge in South Carolina found the termination of roughly $20bn in Greenhouse Gas Reduction Fund grants "arbitrary and capricious and unlawful"; and the DC Circuit ruled 6-4 against the EPA on the GGRF terminations. The EPA's wins were narrower: a Washington state court dismissed a multistate challenge over the $7bn Solar for All programme on jurisdictional grounds, the DC Circuit upheld the EPA's emissions calculation approach for industrial facilities, and a court reaffirmed deadline extensions for steel mill hazardous emissions compliance. Environmental law professor Jody Freeman is quoted on the cost of delay. The EPA is appealing adverse decisions. Why it matters: for an industrial company the takeaway is that the compliance baseline has not moved as far as the policy announcements suggested, and the risk is planning against a rollback that courts then reverse. Capital projects sized to a relaxed standard can end up non-compliant if the prior rule is restored, and retrofits deferred on the strength of an announced rollback are the expensive version of the same mistake — though the remedy matters: a vacatur does not invariably reinstate a predecessor rule, since the outcome depends on what was challenged, the remedy ordered, any stay, and the terms of remand. Two of the EPA's wins are directly relevant to manufacturers — the industrial emissions calculation approach and the steel mill hazardous air pollutant deadline extensions — and both are worth confirming with counsel against actual permits rather than taken from a news summary, since national standards and permit-specific obligations are not the same thing. On coverage: pollution liability wordings distinguish sharply between sudden and accidental releases, gradual pollution, and regulatory fines and penalties, and fines and penalties are frequently excluded or uninsurable as a matter of public policy depending on the state. Cover for the cost of complying with a changed standard is generally not an insurance product at all; it is capital expenditure. On status, the decisions described are trial- and appellate-court rulings of varying posture — one is a jurisdictional dismissal rather than a merits ruling — and with appeals pending none of these outcomes is settled.

Freight's green switch is creating a claims gap for brokers

Insurance Business America, quoting AXA XL underwriting and risk-consulting specialists (carrier commentary) · Aug 25 · fleet / marine
The article describes a widening gap between the pace of freight's energy transition and the repair and coverage infrastructure supporting it. On road, electric vehicles are reported to cost 20–25% more to repair than combustion equivalents, driven by battery replacement, technician shortages and calibration equipment, and the article states around one in five EVs involved in collisions is written off after minor impacts; a Tesla Semi is cited at roughly $290,000. At sea, nine alternative fuel options — LNG, ammonia, battery-electric and nuclear among them — have inconsistent viability across safety, infrastructure and regulatory acceptance. Thomas Saive of AXA XL says electric vehicles introduce a risk profile that differs from traditional fleets; colleague Jarek Klimczak describes the maritime industry as "very confused" about fuel standards; and Jan Scharrer expects alternative fuel bunkering to operate like chemical tankers, with special jetties. Why it matters: directly relevant to any manufacturer electrifying its own fleet or yard equipment, and the mechanism is more specific than "EVs cost more." Repair cost and write-off rate both feed physical-damage loss cost, but the write-off rate is the one that surprises a fleet budget: if a structurally sound vehicle is declared a total loss because the battery pack cannot be economically assessed after an impact, average claim severity rises sharply even with accident frequency flat, and deductibles, valuation basis and agreed-value schedules set for combustion vehicles will be mispriced. Salvage treatment and battery warranty recovery also change the net. The same logic reaches charging infrastructure and lithium-ion storage on site, but the coverage question there is a different one than the article implies: commercial property forms generally exclude or restrict loss caused by mechanical or electrical breakdown, so equipment breakdown coverage is the place to examine charging equipment, alongside fire protection standards for battery storage and the thermal-runaway response plan. Treat the specific figures with care — this is carrier commentary rather than an independent study, the one-in-five write-off statistic is quoted without a named source or geography, and a carrier's underwriters describing a risk as harder is not a neutral observation. The maritime half is longer-dated and reaches manufacturers mainly through carriers' and freight forwarders' costs.

UK firms face exposure to lithium supply disruptions

CIR Magazine, citing research by WTW (broker) with Cullen Hendrix of the Peterson Institute for International Economics · Aug 14 · older but critical · supply chain
WTW, working with the Peterson Institute's Cullen Hendrix, modelled three disruption scenarios affecting the "Lithium Triangle" of Chile, Argentina and Bolivia: an earthquake affecting Bolivian and Argentine production, an export levy imposed by Argentina during economic instability, and a labour dispute at a critical Chilean port. The research finds restricted access, operating restrictions, delayed settlement and non-payment could each become significant sources of financial loss for businesses dependent on batteries, electric vehicles, energy storage and other critical-mineral technologies. Hendrix notes that environmental, political and operational pressures tend to escalate together under uncertainty rather than following a single pathway. Why it matters: the scenario set is the useful part, because it names three different failure modes with three different insurance answers, and two of them are not property perils at all. An earthquake at a producing asset is a physical-damage event upstream, which reaches a buyer only through contingent business interruption — and then only if the cause of loss is covered, the affected supplier falls within how the form treats named, scheduled or unnamed direct suppliers, and territory, waiting-period and period-of-restoration conditions are met; many forms exclude sub-tier suppliers entirely. An export levy is a trade-policy act that standard named-peril political risk cover — expropriation, confiscation, currency inconvertibility, contract frustration — does not reach without specific trade-disruption or change-in-law wording, and even then increased cost alone is often outside the trigger. A port labour dispute causes no physical damage anywhere, so it falls outside conventional property-triggered cover entirely and needs non-damage business interruption or trade-disruption wording. This is a broker's research and therefore also marketing for the products it implies. The genuinely transferable point is cheaper than any of them: know which sub-tier suppliers depend on the Lithium Triangle, because tier-one visibility does not show it — the same limitation the Tier 1 supplier item in Monday's edition described.

NCSC: AI agents need emergency shutdown safeguard

CIR Magazine, reporting the UK National Cyber Security Centre (government agency) · Aug 21 · interim guidance, non-binding, no US legal effect
The NCSC has issued interim advice on deploying autonomous AI systems securely, pending formal guidance being developed with partners. It urges organisations to assess the level of autonomy granted and understand a model's built-in safeguards, maintain tightly managed access, human oversight and robust sandboxing, establish an emergency shutdown capability for rapid intervention if agents misbehave, and bring logging, auditing and monitoring into security operations. The NCSC's stated concern is that the benefits of autonomous systems must be balanced against their ability to act beyond an organisation's intended remit; the reporting cites AI safety assessments in which agents took unauthorised actions, including attempted supply chain attacks against open-source projects. Why it matters: pair this with today's lead item on generative-AI exclusions, because together they describe the same year from two directions — capability is being deployed faster than either the controls or the coverage. Put the legal weight accurately, though. This is interim, non-binding UK guidance with no direct legal effect on a US manufacturer, and it does not become a standard of care by publication. What it can do is supply evidence of prevailing practice: after an incident, a claimant's expert, a regulator or an underwriter may cite it alongside NIST, ISO and industry practice when asking what a reasonable deployment looked like, and its weight depends on jurisdiction and on what other standards say. The concrete item is the one most organisations have not done: an emergency shutdown capability for an agent means a tested kill path, a named owner and a defined authority to use it, not merely the ability to revoke an API key. Note too that the cited incidents come from safety evaluations rather than confirmed attacks in production.

Why it matters: outside a manufacturer's own risk programme and carried only for the market-structure signal — capital continues to flow into vehicles built to assume legacy liabilities, which is the same impulse behind the legacy and run-off market on the property/casualty side. Where that does touch a manufacturer is in the transfer of long-tail liabilities such as historic products, asbestos or environmental exposures, and the distinction there is one that gets muddled and matters. A loss portfolio transfer is ordinarily a reinsurance transaction: it moves reserves and economics but does not sever privity, so the original insurer remains directly liable on those old policies. Only a regulator- or court-approved insurance business transfer, assumption or novation — the UK's Part VII process being the best-known example, with a growing number of US state equivalents — actually changes the counterparty on the policy. If a legacy programme has been through the latter, the entity that answers a claim on an old year is no longer the carrier originally bought from, and that is worth establishing before the claim arrives rather than after.
