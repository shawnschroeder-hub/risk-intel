Aon agrees to acquire USI Insurance from KKR in $17 billion dealAgreed, not closed

Insurance Journal and Insurance Business America · Aug 31 · subject to closing conditions and regulatory approval
$17bn purchase price, $16.7bn net after tax attributes, at 14.5x synergized trailing-twelve-month adjusted EBITDA, funded entirely through new debt, with closing expected in Q4 2026. USI is the tenth-largest US insurance broker — roughly $3bn annual revenue, more than 10,500 employees, nearly 200 offices — offering property and casualty, employee benefits, personal risk and retirement plan advice, primarily to mid-sized businesses. Aon ranks second in US brokerage revenue at $16.99bn behind Marsh McLennan at $26.66bn, and the deal widens Aon's lead over third-place Gallagher. Aon CEO Greg Case says the deal makes Aon "the premier US middle-market platform." Why it matters: the largest brokerage transaction in years, and it belongs at the top of a buyer's brief because broker consolidation reaches buyers through service continuity and market access rather than through price. Status first: this is an agreement, not a completed transaction, and it remains subject to closing conditions and regulatory approval, so nothing has changed yet. If it completes, three consequences are worth thinking about, none certain. Integration disrupts service teams — the account executive, claims advocate and placement specialist who know a programme are the asset, and they are exactly the roles that get rationalised. The realistic response is not to seek a guarantee that cannot be given before closing: it is to document what exists now — the current service obligations in the broker agreement, the key-person dependencies, the transition and termination rights — and then ask for post-close continuity while treating the answer as an intention rather than a commitment. Second, independent broker choice narrows at the top. That is not the same as narrower carrier access, since a combined Aon may retain or expand its market reach, but it does reduce the number of large independent alternatives available to test an incumbent at renewal. Third, an all-debt acquisition of this size raises leverage, and integration cost pressure has historically shown up as service-model standardisation rather than as anything visible in a policy. One conflict question is worth raising early if it applies: where Aon and USI currently serve different entities or towers within the same programme, that arrangement may need revisiting. None of this is a reason to move brokers, which carries substantial costs of its own.

US construction firms face a widening gap between booming property insurance and punishing casualty rates

Risk & Insurance, citing Gallagher's mid-year 2026 industry update (broker research — an interested party) · Aug 31
Construction spending ran at a $2.17tn annualised rate in April 2026. On property, Gallagher reports best-in-class non-catastrophe risks seeing rate decreases of 15–20% or more, and builder's risk single-layer decreases of 5–7%. On casualty, it reports excess liability premiums up 5–30%, general liability up low-to-mid single digits, and workers' compensation flat to +3%. The drivers Gallagher asserts: a labour shortage said to intensify claim severity, with more than 80% of contractors struggling to fill positions; construction defect claims rising from roughly $500,000 to $1.5m–$3m; water loss as a severity multiplier; third-party litigation funding, with private equity backing law firms; and claims delays, with engineer reports now exceeding six weeks against three to four historically. Why it matters: broker research, so the framing is interested, and rate ranges this wide — 5% to 30% on excess liability — describe a market segmenting hard rather than a single number anyone will pay. That width is itself the finding: where the band is this broad, an account's own loss record and submission quality decide where in it the account lands, which argues for investing in the submission rather than shopping the market. Two points of precision before any of it is quoted. Gallagher reports excess liability premium movement, and premium can rise on exposure growth, higher limits or programme changes even where the rate is unchanged — so this is not a clean rate index. And the two halves cannot be netted into a single blended estimate: whether a 15–20% property decrease outweighs a 5–30% casualty increase in actual dollars depends entirely on the relative size of the two premiums, which differ by project, and on who buys each cover. On a large capital project the property and builder's risk premium is frequently the larger of the two, so the arithmetic can run either way. Obtain separate indications and compare real dollars rather than assuming either half dominates. The wider relevance to a manufacturer is that the casualty drivers Gallagher names are not construction-specific — labour shortage, escalating defect severity and litigation funding all describe conditions reaching a company's own products and premises exposure. The defect-claim escalation is a three-to-sixfold rise, characterised by a broker rather than drawn from a published dataset. One connection to Edition 022's wrap-up item, with its condition attached: where a wrap-up is rated on construction value or another inflation-sensitive base, tariff-driven material inflation and rising casualty rates compound — but many wrap-up casualty components rate on payroll or receipts instead, and a manufacturer may not be the purchasing party, so the declarations decide whether this applies.

Larak Island strikes add pressure to an already stressed war-risk market

Insurance Business America · Aug 31 · active conflict · figures are market estimates
US Central Command struck rocket launchers and sea-mine infrastructure on Larak Island in the Strait of Hormuz; Iran's Revolutionary Guard Corps reported two killed and two injured. Iran responded with ballistic missiles at US bases in Jordan, all eight intercepted, and claimed a drone strike on a UAE air base, which the UAE denied while saying it shot down one drone. On the market: claims of $2–3bn are reported across the war, terror and political violence segment against annual premium volume of $1.5–2bn. Hull war-risk premiums were approximately 0.25% of vessel value pre-conflict, surging to as high as 10% at peak, with S&P Global reporting July rates of 7.5–10% of hull value. Dylan Saunders-Mortier of Marsh describes the surge; Neil Roberts of the Lloyd's Market Association says "There was always and there remains sufficient capacity, with cover available for a price." The US International Development Finance Corporation has established a $40bn maritime reinsurance facility with Chubb as lead underwriter alongside Travelers, Liberty Mutual, Berkshire Hathaway, AIG, Starr and CNA. Why it matters: the claims-against-premium comparison is the headline figure and needs handling carefully — it is an indicative stress measure, not a loss ratio. The reporting does not establish whether those claims are paid or incurred, gross or net of reinsurance, or drawn from the same period and portfolio as the premium estimate, so it shows a segment under strain rather than proving anything about underwriting results. What is solid is the price movement: roughly 0.25% to 7.5–10% of hull value is a fortyfold repricing, and Roberts' point that capacity remains available "for a price" is the operationally important one. This is a cost problem, not an availability problem. It is also worth not over-reading the government facility: a $40bn public reinsurance vehicle sits alongside a market the Lloyd's Market Association says still has sufficient capacity, so it may supplement or stabilise private capacity rather than substitute for it, and its attachment point and risk-sharing structure would need to be known before calling it a backstop. For a manufacturer the exposure is almost never the hull — it is in the freight rate and the contract, since war-risk surcharges are passed through by carriers and forwarders. Who bears them is decided by the sale contract and Incoterm, the freight contract and its surcharge clauses, and by who carries transit risk under any cargo or stock-throughput cover; those need reading together rather than assuming the Incoterm settles it. The reporting also does not make clear whether the 7.5–10% figures are voyage additional premiums, quoted maxima or period rates, which materially changes what a transit costs. A second exposure is timing rather than cost: re-routing and convoy waits lengthen lead times, which no marine policy answers.

Fed survey respondents increasingly cite AI risk; Bailey warns of systemic cyber exposure

Insurance Business America, reporting the Federal Reserve's Spring 2026 Financial Stability Report and a Bank of England letter · Aug 31
The Federal Reserve's Spring 2026 Financial Stability Report surveyed approximately 20 market participants; the proportion citing AI as a salient risk to US financial stability rose from 30% in autumn 2025 to 50% in spring 2026, described as one of the sharpest moves in the report's rankings, though geopolitical tensions and oil shocks ranked higher overall. Separately, Andrew Bailey — Bank of England governor and Financial Stability Board chair — wrote to G20 finance ministers that frontier AI's cyber risk impact represents "the single most immediate threat to the global financial system," and that frontier AI models are changing "the speed, scale and economics" of cyberattacks while creating concentration risk through reliance on a small number of dominant cloud and technology providers. Adrien Robinson of Hartford (an insurer) says cyber rates appear "a little disconnected" from the actual risk trajectory. Ed Chadwick of Jencap (a wholesale intermediary) warns AI is shifting claims "from traditional IT failures to more algorithmic risks in general," exposing gaps in legacy wording around third-party AI provider failures. Why it matters: the two signals are not equivalent and should not be quoted as one. The Fed figure is a survey of roughly twenty market participants reporting what worries them — a small panel of opinion, not a central bank's own assessment, and a rise of twenty percentage points on that base. Bailey's letter is the stronger signal, because it is a named regulator's own view rather than a poll of others, and his stated mechanism is the part worth carrying: concentration risk in a small number of cloud and AI providers. That is a related accumulation concern to two others this brief has met in the past fortnight — ISO's casualty AI exclusions and the systemic-event exclusions carriers are drafting for cyber. They address different hazards and triggers and are not the same thing, but they share a worry about many organisations depending on the same few models and platforms, so that one failure is not one claim. For a manufacturer the useful translation is a dependency question rather than an insurance one: which critical processes now route through a single external AI or cloud provider, and what happens for the days that provider is unavailable. Robinson's remark that cyber rates look disconnected from the risk trajectory is an insurer's opinion and cuts against a buyer's short-term interest in a softening line — a reason to treat current cyber pricing as a window rather than a trend, consistent with cyber running down 3.2% in CIAB's Q2 data for a ninth consecutive quarter. Chadwick's point about legacy wording is the concrete renewal question, and it is more than a naming issue: whether a cyber policy's dependent business interruption extension reaches an AI service provider turns on how "dependent provider" is defined, whether indirect as well as direct dependencies qualify, what counts as a qualifying cause of loss, the waiting period and sublimit, and — hardest — whether an algorithmic error or degraded model output is a covered security or system failure at all. Technology errors and omissions cover is worth examining alongside cyber for that last question.

California legislature drops subrogation curb, caps attorney fees and restricts selling claims to private equityNot yet enacted

Insurance Business America · Aug 31 · legislative action — enactment not established in the reporting
Senate Bill 492 would have eliminated insurers' subrogation rights — their ability to sue utilities to recover wildfire claim costs. The bill reached the California Legislature on 29 August 2026, where lawmakers rejected that elimination. The measure as approved instead preserves subrogation rights while imposing new restrictions: capping attorney fees at 10% of settlements and prohibiting the sale of subrogation claims to private equity or hedge funds. The American Property Casualty Insurance Association had warned that eliminating subrogation could raise statewide homeowners' premiums by 10% to 20%. Denni Ritter, an APCIA vice president: "We appreciate that lawmakers put forward a plan that protects Californians and preserves affordability." Governor Newsom called the outcome "real progress" while urging lawmakers to revisit broader liability reform in 2027. Why it matters — and first, on status: the reporting establishes that the Legislature acted and describes the Governor commenting favourably, but it does not state that the bill has been signed or has otherwise become law. Until that is confirmed this is a Legislature-approved measure, not an enacted statute, and the precise text, scope and effective date would all need checking before anyone relies on it. Carried in this section because the two restrictions that survived bear on litigation finance, though the fit needs stating precisely: prohibiting the sale of subrogation claims restricts assignment — the outright transfer of ownership of a claim — which is related to but not the same as third-party litigation funding, where a funder takes a contingent economic interest without necessarily acquiring the claim. Both involve outside capital taking a position in litigation outcomes, and that is the common thread with the disclosure statutes tracked in Editions 021 and 022; the mechanism is different. Combined with a 10% cap on attorney fees, this is a legislature reaching into the economics of a category of litigation rather than its procedure. Two cautions. This is California and wildfire subrogation specifically, so none of it generalises to a manufacturer's products or premises exposure elsewhere. And the 10–20% premium figure was APCIA's projection of what the rejected proposal would have caused — produced by the trade association opposing it, describing a scenario that did not happen, and it should never be quoted as an effect of what the Legislature approved.

Regulators reshape Europe's recall landscapeOlder but critical

CIR Magazine, citing Sedgwick's European Product Safety and Recall Index (vendor research) · Aug 28
7,115 recalls across Europe in H1 2026, a 7.9% decrease from 7,729 in H1 2025, but still the second-highest half-year figure on record. Sector increases: automotive +13.2%, food and beverage +7.6%, toys +5.9%, electronics +3.1%. Sector decreases: clothing −47.9%, plus pharmaceuticals, medical devices and consumer products. Regulatory developments cited: new EU steel tariffs, trade agreements with the US and Australia, proposed UK changes to product safety and market surveillance rules, new food labelling requirements, clearer Notified Bodies expectations and increased advertising scrutiny. Chris Occleshaw, an international product recall consultant at Sedgwick, cautions that a softer overall number should not signal complacency. Why it matters: the European counterpart to the US recall index carried in Edition 021, from the same vendor. One inference worth explicitly not drawing: the European data reports event counts only, with no unit volumes or average recall size, so it cannot be used to show that European recalls are getting larger — that was a US finding and it does not transfer. What the European numbers do support is narrower and still useful: event counts fell 7.9% yet remain historically elevated, and automotive rose 13.2%, the sector figure that matters most to anyone supplying vehicle content and which at least points the same direction as the US automotive data. The regulatory list is the more actionable half, with two qualifications. Notified Bodies are relevant only to product categories whose conformity-assessment route requires their involvement, not everything sold in Europe, so the first question is whether a given component falls into such a category at all. And a European recall is a different commercial event from a US one because notification duties arise under product-specific regimes and national market surveillance authorities rather than through any single channel — the applicable regime for rail and industrial components is what would need identifying, not a continent-wide generalisation. Where a product sells on both sides of the Atlantic, the recall plan has to work under both, and the insurance question that follows is whether the recall policy's territorial scope and defined costs actually reach a European withdrawal.

US expanding long-range commercial drone testing programmeNot a rule change

Insurance Journal · Aug 31 · programme expansion
The US government is broadening drone testing initiatives for advanced long-range commercial applications. Why it matters: carried short and forward-looking, with the limits of the reporting stated plainly — this is a testing programme being widened, not a change to current operating restrictions, and nothing about what operators may do today is stated to have changed. Long-range operation beyond visual line of sight is the regulatory unlock that would make drones broadly useful on large industrial sites: outdoor yard inventory, stockpile measurement, roof and stack inspection, perimeter and rail-siding survey. The near-term interest for a manufacturer is a safety one. Inspections flown rather than climbed may reduce work at height, and falls remain among the most severe workplace injury categories — though "reduce" is the right word rather than "eliminate", since many inspections still require someone on the structure, and drones introduce their own aviation, privacy and cyber exposures. Two insurance points worth settling before any operational pilot, because that is when they are cheap to solve. Commercial drone operations sit awkwardly in a conventional programme: a general liability policy commonly carries an aircraft exclusion which can reach unmanned aircraft depending on the wording, so cover is usually arranged specifically rather than assumed. And using a contracted operator changes the analysis without removing it — premises liability, negligent selection and vicarious liability exposures remain with the site owner — so the practical checklist is aviation liability limits, indemnity provisions, additional insured status where available, operator credentials, and clarity on who holds responsibility for airspace authorisations.

Tokio Marine HCC to acquire Direct Commercial LimitedOlder but critical

CIR Magazine · Aug 28 · terms undisclosed
Tokio Marine HCC International has agreed to acquire Direct Commercial Limited, a UK commercial motor managing general agent. Why it matters: a small transaction carried for the pattern it belongs to rather than for itself. It is the second acquisition in today's brief alongside Aon and USI, and consolidation is running at both ends of the market at once. The direction worth noting is that a large carrier is buying distribution: a managing general agent holds delegated underwriting authority and, more importantly, holds the data and relationships for a specialist book. Carriers acquiring MGAs is how underwriting expertise in a niche gets internalised, and commercial motor is a line under severity pressure in most markets, which makes it a plausible thing to want to control directly rather than delegate. The read-across for a buyer is modest and should not be overstated: DCL already writes on carrier capacity, so a change of ownership does not by itself remove an underwriting option from the market, and the operation may continue much as before. What may diminish is its independence, and in niches where the number of genuinely distinct underwriting appetites is already small, that is the thing to watch at renewal. Nothing here affects a US manufacturer's programme.
