Why the P&C insurance market's soft rates mask coverage risk

Insurance Business America, citing Alera Group (retail broker) · Aug 21 · broker commentary
Justin Foa, executive vice president and national property and casualty practice leader at Alera Group, reports commercial property premiums down about 4% in H1 2026 and umbrella and excess liability up about 11%, and argues insurers are offsetting price reductions by tightening policy language and adding exclusions. He cites nuclear verdicts — awards above $10m — rising to 149 in 2025 from 44 in 2020, and notes that risk purchasing groups which once offered $100m of cover for a few thousand dollars have largely disappeared. Why it matters: a retail broker describing the value of retail broking, so the conclusion is interested — but the mechanism is real and checkable at any renewal. His property figure is broker-reported and does not reconcile with either Q2 dataset carried in Edition 017: CIAB has property premiums falling 6.3%, Ivans has property renewal rates still rising 6.40%. Three measures of three different populations over two different periods, and none of them describes a particular buyer's schedule. What survives all three is the liability direction — umbrella is up on every one. The actionable version is narrow: ask for a marked-up comparison of expiring against proposed wording, and price every new exclusion, sublimit or deductible change against what it removes. A credit accepted for an exclusion is only a saving if the excluded exposure is genuinely negligible for that operation, which is the buyer's judgment rather than the underwriter's.

Abbott agrees to $670 million settlement of infant formula cases

Insurance Journal / Associated Press · Aug 21 · products liability · partial settlement
Abbott agreed to pay $670m to resolve roughly 2,000 claims held by three law firms, alleging that its specialty formulas for preterm infants caused necrotising enterocolitis, a serious bowel disease affecting premature newborns. Abbott did not admit liability, called the deal "a step toward substantially resolving the overall litigation," and still faces claims from as many as 12,700 people. Why it matters: the figure to carry is not $670m but the ratio — about 2,000 claims settled against up to 12,700 potential claimants — which means the exposure has been partly priced, not closed. A settlement agreement is also not a final resolution; such arrangements commonly remain subject to participation thresholds and, in aggregated proceedings, court involvement. The transferable question is limit structure rather than long-tail cover. NEC is an acute injury arising in neonatal care, so each claim generally attaches to the policy year of injury — this is not an asbestos-style continuous-trigger problem. The relevant technical issues are the definition of "occurrence," whether a batch clause aggregates injuries traced to one production run, how the products-completed operations aggregate behaves under a wave of claims, whether defence costs sit inside or outside limits, and how a tower erodes when many claims settle at once. All answerable from a company's own policy wording in an afternoon.

UK factories face rising cyber disruption riskOlder but critical

CIR Magazine, citing Make UK's Cyber Security in Manufacturing report (manufacturers' trade body) · Aug 12 · survey research
Make UK reports that 30% of the manufacturers it surveyed suffered a serious cyber incident directly or through their supply chain, while 70% avoided one. Among those affected, 46% reported increased operating costs and production downtime, 31% supply-chain delays, 31% reduced capacity and 23% component or material shortages. The report identifies persistent weaknesses in patching, incident-response planning, executive accountability and recovery testing. Why it matters: a trade body surveying its own UK members, so the percentages are directional rather than a benchmark — who answers a cyber survey is self-selecting in both directions. The useful part is the shape of the loss, not its frequency. Every consequence reported is operational — downtime, delays, reduced capacity, shortages — not privacy or notification costs. That determines which parts of a cyber policy actually matter: the business-interruption section, the system-failure trigger, the waiting period, the restoration and extended indemnity periods, and any dependent business-interruption extension, rather than the breach-response machinery that dominates cyber marketing. The supply-chain half deserves separating properly. A cyber policy's dependent BI extension typically responds to a security or system failure at a qualifying dependency, and may or may not require that supplier to be named; a property policy's contingent BI wording generally requires physical loss or damage at the dependent property, so a supplier hit by ransomware with no physical damage may fall outside it entirely. Which responds, or neither, is a wording question best asked before renewal. Worth adding that OT segmentation, tested offline backups and a stated recovery-time objective do more to limit this loss than any policy term.

TPA governance risk hides in vendors' AI control, requires carrier strategy

Insurance Business America, citing Baker Tilly (advisory firm) and the Ncontracts 2026 State of Third-Party Risk Management Survey (vendor research) · Aug 21
John Romano, a principal at Baker Tilly, argues the governance problem is not AI itself but the absence of visibility into where vendors have deployed it: the Ncontracts survey reports 72% of institutions cannot fully identify which of their vendors use artificial intelligence. Romano proposes replacing a uniform "toll booth" compliance model with an "express lane" that expedites low-risk uses while applying heightened oversight where AI affects price, coverage, claims outcomes, fraud decisions or regulated customer communications. His decisive test is whether a vendor merely advises or holds actual decision authority. Why it matters: the article is about carriers and their third-party administrators and the survey population is financial institutions, so the 72% does not describe a manufacturer's vendor base and should not be quoted as if it did. The framework is what transfers. The advise-versus-decide test is the right question for any vendor with authority over an outcome the company remains accountable for — the TPA adjudicating workers' compensation claims, a benefits administrator, a logistics provider selecting motor carriers, a supplier's automated quality inspection. Two consequences: contracts written before generative AI was in general use are unlikely to address it, so the clauses to check are right of audit, notification of material process change, data-use restrictions, human review of adverse decisions and record retention; and where a vendor's AI drives a decision, the company's own legal and regulatory responsibility generally does not transfer merely because a tool made it — though indemnities, liability caps and required vendor insurance can allocate the economic loss.

Multistate salmonella infections climb to 431, CDC says

Insurance Journal, citing the CDC · Aug 21 · active outbreak investigation
The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention reported that a salmonella outbreak linked to jalapeño peppers has now sickened 431 people across multiple states. Why it matters: not a manufacturing story on its face, and carried for one reason — it is a live illustration of the exposure a general liability policy is worst at covering. Contamination reaching consumers generates bodily-injury claims that products liability is designed for, but the cost that usually dominates such an event is the withdrawal itself: identifying affected lots, transporting, storing and destroying product, and replacing it. In the standard ISO commercial general liability form that first-party cost is addressed by the recall exclusion — exclusion (n), "Recall of Products, Work or Impaired Property," the one commonly called the sistership exclusion. Naming it precisely matters, because it is routinely confused with the separate impaired-property exclusion (m), which deals with third-party property damage where the insured's product has not physically injured anything. Recall cost requires its own product recall or contaminated-products policy, and the triggers are more varied than "mandatory versus voluntary" suggests — accidental contamination, malicious tampering, product extortion, and whether the withdrawal was reasonable and necessary all appear in different wordings. For a components manufacturer, where a part forces a customer's recall of their finished good, a standard first-party recall policy may not respond at all: the relevant covers are third-party recall expense, customer financial loss or product-guarantee wordings. Traceability and lot identification determine what any of them cost. Case counts in an active investigation typically rise as testing catches up, so 431 is a point in time.

Nuclear verdict counts: 149 verdicts totalling $25.1bn in 2025Older but critical

Litigation Sentinel (commercial verdict tracker) · 2025 full-year data
Litigation Sentinel's tracking counted 149 nuclear verdicts in 2025 totalling $25.1bn across 28 states. The same 149 figure appears in the Alera Group commentary above, set against 44 in 2020. Why it matters: carried to put a number behind the sentiment above, with the caveat that this is a commercial tracker's count rather than an official statistic — there is no authoritative national register of verdicts by size, so every count depends on what its compiler captures and how it handles definitions. Three cautions apply to any nuclear-verdict figure now in circulation, this one included. A verdict is not a judgment and neither is a payment: large awards are frequently reduced on post-trial motion, remittitur or appeal, and tracked totals generally do not follow them down, so gross verdicts overstate insured and paid losses by an unknown margin. The 44-to-149 span covers a period of pandemic court closures followed by backlog clearance, so part of the increase is trial timing rather than jury behaviour. And a headline award may include punitive damages, whose insurability varies by state. The number is directionally useful and precisely unreliable, which is exactly how it should be quoted — as evidence of a trend in reported awards, not a measure of what carriers actually pay.

Artemis (specialist ILS trade publication) · week ending Aug 16, with a Hannover Re item dated Aug 20
Artemis reports that third-party reinsurance capital deployed through catastrophe bonds, insurance-linked securities and other alternative vehicles is projected to grow roughly 6% across 2026 to end near a record $130bn, and reports Hannover Re Capital Partners building out its operations and broadening its investor base as of 20 August. Why it matters: a projection reported by a trade publication, not an audited result, and it describes reinsurance capital rather than anything a manufacturer buys directly. It is carried because it is one of the mechanisms behind the property capacity the CIAB survey reported this week: more capital competing for catastrophe risk lowers the reinsurance cost carriers build into primary property pricing. That link is real but loose, lagged and partial — primary property pricing also reflects loss experience, insured values, attachment points, carrier appetite and non-catastrophe perils, so alternative capital explains part of the picture rather than all of it, and no renewal outcome should be predicted from it. It can also reverse, though not instantly: a major catastrophe can impair cat bond principal or trap collateral, and how quickly capacity actually withdraws depends on loss development, investor reloading and new issuance. Artemis's own site was not directly reachable today, so this is drawn from indexed summaries of its reporting rather than the article pages, and the $130bn should be quoted as Artemis's projection.

Willis launches parametric product for Caribbean coral reefsOlder, carried for structure

Insurance Edge, reporting on WTW · Aug 6
Willis designed a pilot parametric policy for the 2026–27 Atlantic hurricane season covering roughly 1,800 km² of coral reef along the coasts of the Dominican Republic, Jamaica, St Lucia, and St Vincent and the Grenadines. The structure introduces a "Dynamic Cat-in-Circle" trigger which the reporting says considerably reduces the near-miss problem present in traditional Cat-in-Circle policies. Why it matters: the subject matter is irrelevant to a manufacturer and the trigger design is not. Basis risk — the mismatch between what a parametric policy pays and what was actually lost — is the central objection to these structures, and it runs in both directions. The trigger can fail to fire when a real loss occurs (the classic near miss, where a hurricane passes just outside the circle and a damaged business collects nothing), and it can also fire when little or no loss occurred, which is not a windfall so much as a cost priced into everyone's premium. A design that narrows the first gap is worth watching, because parametric structures are most attractive precisely where indemnity cover is hard to obtain or slow to pay — a plant in a catastrophe-exposed location, or a supplier concentration in one. Carried as a technical development to follow, not a product to consider: one pilot on coral reefs does not establish that the structure performs, and reducing near misses does not by itself prove overall basis risk is lower.

UK firms face exposure to lithium supply disruptions

CIR Magazine, citing WTW (broker) with Cullen Hendrix of the Peterson Institute for International Economics · Aug 14 · scenario research
WTW, working with Cullen Hendrix of the Peterson Institute, modelled three disruption scenarios for firms dependent on lithium: an earthquake affecting operations in Bolivia and Argentina, an export levy imposed by Argentina under economic stress, and a labour dispute at a critical Chilean port. The research describes resulting losses through restricted access, operating restrictions, delayed settlement and non-payment. Hendrix observes that in complex operating environments like the Lithium Triangle, disruption rarely follows a single pathway, and that environmental, political and operational pressures tend to escalate together. Why it matters: a broker publishing scenario research with a policy institute — the scenarios are illustrative constructions, not forecasts, and no probability is attached to any of them. The transferable finding is the one in the quotation: geographic concentration converts unrelated hazard types into a single correlated exposure. A supplier concentration in one region is simultaneously a natural-catastrophe, political-risk and labour exposure, and a conventional risk register usually records those in three separate places owned by three different people. For a manufacturer with battery, electrification or energy-storage content in its products or its plant, the useful exercise is to list critical inputs by country of origin rather than by supplier name, which is how a concentration of this kind becomes visible at all. A caution on the insurance response, because it is easy to overstate: political risk cover responds to specified perils such as expropriation, confiscation, currency inconvertibility or contract frustration, and does not ordinarily respond to a generally applicable tax, tariff or export levy; trade credit insurance covers non-payment of insured receivables by buyers, not a manufacturer's own increased input costs; property and contingent BI generally require physical damage at a covered location. A port labour dispute causing delay without damage most often falls to trade disruption or non-damage supply chain insurance, which is a real if specialised market. The operational substitutes — inventory buffers, dual sourcing, alternate ports and routes, and force majeure and price-adjustment clauses — usually do more work than any of them.

Small businesses absorb double-digit health insurance hikes with little insight into costs

Risk & Insurance, citing an Ignition Benefits survey (vendor research) · Aug 21 · employee benefits
A survey of 503 leaders at US companies with 10–250 employees reports 39% saw double-digit renewal increases and 9% saw increases of 20% or more, against an overall average of 7%. 64% made budget tradeoffs and 26% cut raises or bonuses; 48% paid at or near the insurer's first quote. Only 31% received a clear breakdown of what drove their increase, falling to 24% among C-suite respondents. 38% reviewed two or fewer quotes and 47% lacked confidence they were paying a fair rate. On GLP-1 drugs, 58% of plans covering them could not quantify the cost and 63% said they would restrict coverage if costs rose a further 6–14%. Only 6% had reviewed their broker's performance. Why it matters: vendor research by a benefits firm whose service is the remedy the findings imply, and the sample is small employers, so the percentages do not describe a large manufacturer's plan. Two findings survive that framing, and one correction is worth making to how a brief like this is tempted to wave the topic away. This is not purely a benefits-governance matter that happens to reach the CFO through the budget: a large manufacturer's medical plan is typically self-funded and sits behind specific and aggregate medical stop-loss insurance, so a surge in high-cost claims and specialty drug spend feeds directly into stop-loss attachment points, the lasering of known claimants and stop-loss renewal pricing. That is an insurance-programme question, and in many organisations risk management owns it. The two transferable findings: the cost-driver transparency gap — 31% receiving a clear explanation, senior executives least often — is a question any employer can put to its own administrator regardless of size, namely what specifically drove the increase in claims terms; and GLP-1 exposure is a live, fast-moving and genuinely hard-to-project line item, which is precisely the profile that stresses an aggregate stop-loss attachment.

Heat risk threatens London's economic resilience

CIR Magazine, citing a report from the Mayor of London and London Councils (government bodies) · Aug 21 · adaptation report
The report estimates climate-related damage to London's economy at £16bn–£36bn a year by the 2050s, roughly 3% of the city's economy, and sets out 37 priorities across buildings, business, emergency preparedness, green space, health and care, and critical infrastructure. It cites the 2022 heatwave, when rail buckling caused cancellations and speed restrictions, water consumption rose 50%, and two datacentre failures at Guy's and St Thomas' hospitals disrupted patient care at a cost of £1.4m in unplanned spending. Professor Emma Howard Boyd notes the damage figure is more than double the estimated £16bn annual cost of reaching net zero across the whole UK. Why it matters: a government adaptation report making the case for adaptation spending, and a 2050s projection with a range wide enough — £16bn to £36bn, a factor of more than two — to show how uncertain the modelling is. Quote the range, never the top of it. What is not a projection is the 2022 evidence, and that is the part worth carrying: the failures described were rail infrastructure buckling and datacentre cooling failing, which is an operational-continuity profile rather than a building-damage one. That matters because a heat-driven production halt with no physical damage generally sits outside a conventional property business-interruption policy by design, not by oversight — but "outside the property policy" is not the same as uninsurable. Non-damage business interruption wordings, parametric temperature products, utility and service-interruption extensions and equipment breakdown cover each address part of this exposure, with availability and breadth varying considerably. For a manufacturer the first questions are process-specific and answerable internally: which operations have a temperature ceiling, whether cooling and power have redundancy, and what happens to inbound and outbound logistics when rail or road networks impose heat restrictions.
