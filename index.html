Top Stories
  1. Market — two Q2 datasets landed today and they point in opposite directions, which is itself the finding: the softening is real but narrow, not a market-wide turn. The Council of Insurance Agents & Brokers, a broker trade association surveying its own members, reports average premiums across all accounts down 2%, large accounts down 3.7%, commercial property down 6.3%, cyber and workers' compensation each down 3.2%, with 75% of respondents seeing more property capacity. Umbrella rose 5.3% — a 35th consecutive quarterly increase — and commercial auto 4.5%. But the Ivans Index, which measures renewal rate change on business flowing through agency management systems and skews to small and mid-market accounts, still shows commercial property rising 6.40%, merely slower than Q1's 6.83%. Both can be true, and the most likely reconciliation is account size and method — though neither publisher says so, so treat that as inference rather than fact. Three cautions before this is repeated to anyone. Where a property schedule sits between those two buckets determines whether there is relief at all. Premium is not rate: changes in values, limits and structure move premium independently. And a softening market delivers price relief bundled with narrower wordings, higher deductibles and new sublimits at least as often as not, so any improvement has to be read against terms, not just the number. Workers' compensation is soft on both measures, which is the one unambiguous good news.
  2. Liability — a unanimous Supreme Court ruling has stripped freight brokers of a preemption defence, and the practical question it raises is who picks the carriers. In Montgomery v. Caribe Transport II the Court unanimously rejected freight brokers' federal preemption defence, allowing state-law negligent-hiring claims against brokers to proceed. Precision matters here: preemption is not immunity, and permitting a claim to proceed decides nothing about negligence or damages in any case. The reporting cites a $604m jury verdict recommendation against C.H. Robinson from a fatal 2021 collision — a recommendation, not an entered judgment — brokers sharply contracting carrier networks, and double-digit increases in transportation insurance premiums. This creates no new direct liability for a manufacturer that ships goods, and it should not be presented as if it does. The economic effect is that brokered freight gets more expensive and the usable carrier pool narrows, though whether that cost lands on brokers, carriers, shippers or consumers is genuinely unsettled. The durable point is the negligent-selection theory itself: it turns on who chose the motor carrier and on what basis. Where a shipper selects carriers itself, or a contract puts that choice on it, the questions are what vetting standard applies, whether it is documented, and what the contracts say about indemnity, additional-insured status and evidence of insurance.
  3. Supply chain — exposure mapping probably stops one tier too early, and reported tariff relief does not undo the reroutes already made. Deloitte's Center for Integrated Research reports that about 33% of executives it surveyed in 2025 had a natural disaster or severe weather event affect their business, that around one-fifth of companies have a climate adaptation plan and roughly half assess supplier risk exposure at all, and that such analysis commonly stops at tier-1 suppliers — citing a 2021 Texas semiconductor shutdown that cost one company an estimated $100m. Separately, US–Canada tariff reductions are reported as potential rather than confirmed — vehicles from 25% to 15% and steel and aluminium from 50% to 25% — with no effective date established in the reporting, so no claims-cost conclusion should assume they take effect. Nationwide's point is the durable one: lower tariffs will not reverse supply-chain decisions already taken, even if some reroutes eventually reverse on their own. This runs straight into yesterday's correction on dependent-property cover. Contingent business interruption generally requires physical loss or damage at a qualifying dependent property and, depending on wording, may be limited to scheduled suppliers or extend on a sublimited blanket basis. So a tier-2 dependency nobody has mapped may or may not be covered — and that is exactly the point: nobody currently knows. Mapping the tiers and reading the dependent-property wording are one project, and neither answers the question alone.

CIAB survey: overall soft market conditions continue in Q2

Insurance Journal, citing the Council of Insurance Agents & Brokers (broker trade association) · Aug 20 · survey research
Average premiums across all accounts fell 2% in Q2 2026 — large accounts down 3.7%, medium down 1.9%, small down 0.5%. By line: commercial property down 6.3%, cyber down 3.2%, workers' compensation down 3.2%; umbrella up 5.3%, a 35th consecutive quarterly increase, and commercial auto up 4.5%. 75% of respondents noted an increase in property capacity, identified in the survey as downward pressure on premium as carriers competed. Why it matters: a trade association surveying its own broker members, so it reports what brokers say they achieved rather than an audited index, and the large-account band is the most volatile because a few big placements move the average. Note also that this measures premium, not rate — changes in insured values, limits and programme structure move premium on their own. The split between property and cyber softening on capacity while umbrella and commercial auto keep rising on severity is the durable observation, and it argues for treating the two halves of a programme differently this year. It says nothing about terms, which is where a soft market usually gives back some of what it offers on price.

Commercial insurance rate increases cool across most lines in second quarter

Risk & Insurance, citing the Ivans Index (vendor data) · Aug 19 · 1 day old
Average premium renewal rate change fell quarter over quarter for every major commercial line except workers' compensation: umbrella 7.96% against 9.36% in Q1, commercial auto 4.93% against 5.28%, BOP 6.16% against 6.74%, general liability 5.44% against 6.85%, commercial property 6.40% against 6.83%, and workers' compensation at −1.37% against −1.73%. Why it matters: this dataset and the CIAB survey above appear to contradict each other, and the contradiction is worth surfacing rather than smoothing. Ivans shows every line except workers' compensation still rising, only more slowly; CIAB shows most lines falling. The likeliest explanation is what each measures — Ivans records renewal rate change on policies flowing through agency management systems, weighted to small and mid-market business, while CIAB surveys brokers about the accounts they place, which skews larger — but neither publisher makes that reconciliation, so it is inference. The practical consequence is that where a property schedule sits between those two populations determines which number describes the renewal. "Property is down 6.3%" should not travel into a budget conversation without that caveat.

US–Canada tariff cuts could ease auto and property claims costsReported, not confirmed

Insurance Business America, citing Nationwide · Aug 20 · carrier commentary
On the reporting, Canadian vehicle tariffs may fall from 25% to 15% and steel and aluminium duties from 50% to 25%. The article cites a spring 2025 estimate of $30–61bn in potential added personal auto claim costs over twelve months, notes personal auto is nearly a third of US property and casualty premium volume, and reports US reconstruction costs up 3.6% between April 2025 and April 2026, down from 5.2% previously. It also cites 49% of businesses renegotiating or diversifying supply chains, with only 3% describing their chains as very resilient. Why it matters: status first — the reporting describes these reductions as prospective, without an effective date, covered-goods list or origin rules, so nothing should be budgeted on them. Even if they take effect, lower headline tariff percentages do not translate directly into lower claims costs, because scope, exemptions, exchange rates, existing inventory and underlying material prices all sit in between. The $30–61bn figure is a year-old projection about personal auto, which is not a manufacturer's exposure, and is carried only as an indication of scale. The durable point is yesterday's: tariff relief does not un-reroute a supply chain, and the insurance consequence sits in the dependent-property schedule rather than the tariff line.

Climate risk analysis rarely reaches beyond tier 1 suppliers

Risk & Insurance, citing the Deloitte Center for Integrated Research (consultancy research), with supplementary data from S&P Global and CDP · Aug 20
About 33% of global executives Deloitte surveyed in 2025 reported natural disasters and severe weather affecting their business. Around one-fifth of companies have a climate adaptation action plan and roughly half assess supplier risk exposure. The research observes that physical climate risks frequently originate outside a company's direct operations yet cascade through interconnected value chains, and cites a 2021 Texas semiconductor plant shutdown estimated to have caused $100m of disruption to a single company. It proposes a five-part resilience framework covering risk mapping, operational adjustment, financial planning, governance and regulatory adaptation. Why it matters: consultancy research promoting a framework the firm sells, so the framework is worth less than the underlying finding, and the survey population shifts between executives surveyed and companies generally, so the percentages are not all measuring the same base. The finding that survives is that supplier climate analysis commonly stops at tier 1 while the dependencies that halt production can sit further down — a sole-source supplier's own sole-source input. That is the exposure counterpart to yesterday's coverage point: contingent business interruption generally requires physical damage at a qualifying dependent property, and whether an unmapped tier-2 dependency is covered depends on whether the wording is scheduled, blanket or unnamed. The honest position is that most buyers do not currently know, and finding out requires the mapping and the wording together.

A landmark Supreme Court ruling is upending how America moves its goods

Insurance Journal / Bloomberg · Aug 20 · Supreme Court ruling
In Montgomery v. Caribe Transport II the Supreme Court unanimously rejected freight brokers' federal preemption defence, allowing state-law negligent-hiring claims against brokers to proceed on motor-vehicle safety claims. The reporting cites a $604m jury verdict recommendation against C.H. Robinson from a Texas case involving a fatal 2021 Mississippi collision, brokers sharply contracting carrier networks — one reducing from 15,000–18,000 carriers to about 8,000 — strong double-digit increases in transportation insurance premiums, and significant declines in major brokers' stock values since the verdict was disclosed. A transportation attorney quoted in the piece observed that higher freight insurance costs "all go somewhere. Usually they end up on the consumer." Why it matters: the most consequential item today, and the one most easily over-read. Two precisions. Preemption is not immunity — the Court removed a federal defence, it did not create liability, and allowing claims to proceed decides nothing about negligence or damages in any individual case. And the $604m is described as a jury verdict recommendation rather than an entered judgment, with post-trial posture unstated. What reaches a manufacturer is economic and contractual rather than legal: brokered freight will cost more and the usable carrier pool is contracting, though where that cost ultimately settles is unsettled. The negligent-selection question — who chose the carrier, on what basis, and documented how — is the one worth answering internally, together with what transportation contracts say about indemnity, additional-insured status and evidence of insurance.

Zuckerberg did not make child safety a priority at Meta, former engineer testifiesAllegation, not finding

Insurance Journal / Reuters · Aug 20 · trial testimony
Former Meta engineering director Arturo Bejar testified in a federal trial in Oakland, California, in a suit brought by a coalition of 29 states led by California, Colorado, Kentucky and New Jersey, alleging Meta designed Facebook and Instagram to addict young users and illegally collected children's data. Bejar, at Meta from 2009–2015 and 2019–2021, testified that metrics rewarded engagement over safety and characterised a break-reminder tool as designed to fail. The trial is expected to run six weeks, Zuckerberg is expected to testify, and Meta has denied the allegations. Why it matters: one witness's testimony in a trial that has not concluded — evidence of an allegation, not a finding, and nothing here is established. It is carried as the live test of the "addictive design" theory this brief covered on 11 August, where the alleged wrongful act is a deliberate design choice rather than a defect. One correction to how that was framed previously: an intentional design decision does not by itself defeat the occurrence requirement in a commercial general liability policy, because an intended act can still cause unintended injury. Whether cover responds turns on the expected-or-intended exclusion, the specific allegations and the jurisdiction — and in practice technology E&O, cyber, privacy and D&O wordings are more likely to be in play than CGL. For a manufacturer the connection is connected consumer products with engagement features. Watch the outcome; do not act on testimony.

Litigation funding — a federal rules amendment, not the bill, is the route to watch

Congress.gov for the bill; the Rule 26 proposal filed by the Institute for Legal Reform (tort-reform advocacy body) and Lawyers for Civil Justice · bill introduced February 2026 · proposal filed March 10, 2026
The Litigation Funding Transparency Act of 2026 (S.3826) remains introduced and pending in the Senate Judiciary Committee since February — no committee vote, no floor action, no enactment. Separately, the US Chamber Institute for Legal Reform and Lawyers for Civil Justice filed a joint proposal on 10 March asking that Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 26(a)(1)(A) be amended to require disclosure, at the outset of a case, of any non-party funder with a financial interest and the agreements defining it. Why it matters: a correction to how this brief has framed the issue. For several weeks the item tracked S.3826 and reported no movement, which was accurate but incomplete — a Federal Rules amendment is the other route to a federal disclosure requirement, and it does not need Congress to pass anything. It does need a great deal else: a private proposal is a request, not a pending amendment, and even if the Advisory Committee on Civil Rules takes it up it runs through committee consideration, publication for public comment, the Judicial Conference, the Supreme Court and a congressional review window — a process measured in years. So nothing has changed for any matter currently on foot. The reason to track it is that disclosure, if it ever arrives, would change settlement dynamics in funded litigation, though even that is qualified: agreements may be produced under protective order or in redacted form, and identifying a funder does not reveal what return it needs.

Soft inland marine market opens door to higher limits as equipment values surge

Insurance Business America, citing Amwins Program Underwriters (wholesale broker and program underwriter) · Aug 20
Jason Baynard, senior vice president at Amwins Program Underwriters, describes expanded carrier capacity and reduced pricing in inland marine. Three trends: data centre construction driving equipment concentrations in temporary storage, with temporary storage limits potentially reaching $30–50m and warehouse legal liability cover coming into play; tariffs materially raising replacement cost, illustrated by an $8m crane that incurred an additional $1.4m in tariff costs; and growth in loss-of-use cover providing monthly payments when key equipment is unavailable. Why it matters: a wholesale broker describing a market it sells into, so the enthusiasm is interested — but the timing observation is checkable and real. Inland marine covers movable equipment and property in transit: mobile plant, tooling moving between sites, contractors' equipment, property at temporary locations. Two things have moved at once — capacity has loosened, and tariffs have raised what the same machine costs to replace — which is the argument for revisiting values and limits now rather than at renewal, since a schedule valued before the tariff round is understated against current replacement cost. Expect additional limit to carry additional premium; a soft market makes the increase easier to obtain, not free. Two technical notes: warehouse legal liability protects a bailee against liability for others' property, so it is relevant to storing customer goods, not as cover for one's own equipment in storage. And loss-of-use is not a substitute for equipment breakdown insurance, which is the standard cover for the failure of a single critical machine and may already sit in the property programme — the question is how the two interact, not whether to buy one instead.

Medicare's 2026 fee schedule shifts signal modest but uneven pressure on workers' comp costs

Risk & Insurance, citing NCCI (workers' compensation rating and data organisation) · Aug 19 · 1 day old
NCCI's analysis reports a 3.3% increase in the physician conversion factor, a 2.6% facility payment increase and a 2.0% DMEPOS fee schedule update. Physician costs are about 40% of workers' compensation medical spend and facility costs about 40%, with durable medical equipment ranging from 4% to 13% by state. NCCI assesses these federal adjustments as "not expected to be a significant source of upward pressure on overall workers' compensation medical costs," with impact varying by state depending on how closely each state's fee schedule tracks CMS rates. Why it matters: higher fee schedule amounts mean providers are paid more, so the direction is a modest headwind on workers' compensation medical costs — upward pressure, not relief. NCCI's own conclusion is that the aggregate effect should not be significant, which is the honest summary, and NCCI is the industry's rating and data organisation, so it is technically authoritative on this without being disinterested. The practical angle for a multi-state manufacturer is the state variation: states that peg their workers' compensation fee schedule closely to Medicare will pass this through and states that set their own will not, so the effect on loss costs depends entirely on the state mix. One question to the broker or TPA about which states track CMS would answer it. Note this is medical cost only and says nothing about indemnity costs or claim frequency.
