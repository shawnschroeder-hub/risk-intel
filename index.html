CFO Brief — three takeaways, written to be read aloud
  1. Severity — the value being stolen is rising even as the number of thefts falls. Cargo theft incidents dropped 26% in the second quarter against the same quarter last year, but the value of goods reported stolen more than doubled to $304.6m from $135.7m, across 677 incidents — an implied average near $450,000 per event, on Verisk CargoNet data reported by Insurance Journal. Note this is the value of goods taken, not insured claim severity; deductibles, valuation terms and recoveries all sit between the two. The exposure for a manufacturer is in-transit finished goods and high-value inputs. The question worth putting to a broker is not just whether transit and stock throughput limits are sized against current shipment and accumulation values, but whether the wording actually covers theft by fraudulent pickup — where a load is handed over to someone posing as the booked carrier. That loss mechanism runs into voluntary-parting and trick-or-device exclusions in many cargo forms, and it is the mechanism most consistent with fewer thefts taking far more value.
  2. Market — this month's derecho is not expected to move property rates. HUB International's North American property practice leader told Insurance Business: "Given where the market is (soft stage of the cycle), this event is not likely to reverse or even stabilize current overall property rate behavior on its own." He set that against North American severe convective storm insured losses of $46bn in 2025, $59bn in 2023 and $52bn in 2024 at 2025 prices. That is one broker's indication of market direction, not a forecast of what any individual account renews at. It answers the question yesterday's edition left open, when a Midwest storm outbreak was flagged as a possible top-ten industry loss. Two carriers' own numbers point the same way today — QBE reported a group-wide renewal rate increase of just 0.3% for the half, down from 2.1%. The planning line is that competitive property terms should still be available, but that underwriters look at repeat storm-loss locations individually, through deductibles, sub-limits and valuation conditions as well as rate.
  3. Exposure — a supplier's cyber incident can reach a manufacturer as a liability claim, not only as lost production. Risk & Insurance reports that a March 2026 attack on an automotive breathalyzer technology provider affected 15,000–30,000 vehicles of roughly 150,000 users, with estimated aggregate availability losses of $7.5m–$60m and potential class-action exposure above $100m. Those are analytical estimates spanning a wide range, not settled claims. The structural point is what matters: the supplier's outage disabled a function in products already sold, so the exposure presents as third-party claims against the manufacturer — in warranty, consumer-protection or tort — rather than as its own business interruption loss. Separately, Make UK found nearly 30% of UK manufacturers suffered a serious cyber incident directly or through their supply chain. Where a product depends on a third-party service to function, the coverage question needs asking before an incident, not after.

Derecho losses mount, but property market unlikely to turn, says broker

Insurance Business America, citing broker HUB International · Aug 14 · broker commentary
HUB International executive vice president and North American property practice leader Blake J. Giannisis said the derecho is unlikely to change property pricing on its own, citing North American severe convective storm insured losses of $46bn in 2025, $59bn in 2023 and $52bn in 2024 at 2025 prices. He said a major hurricane or earthquake in a heavily insured area could shift conditions where a single derecho will not, and that underwriters may look more closely at accounts with repeated severe convective storm exposure. Why it matters: a broker describing his own market, with an interest in clients feeling the market is competitive — read it as a market indication rather than a prediction of any particular renewal. It does settle the question yesterday's edition left open about the Midwest outbreak: severe convective storm has become a large and regularly recurring annual loss that carriers and reinsurers plan around, so a single event of this kind is not what reprices a market. That said, "budgeted for" does not mean "immune" — sustained aggregate losses can still move attachment points, catastrophe capacity and regional appetite. The part that touches a manufacturer directly is the second half: repeat storm losses at the same locations get priced individually, through wind and hail deductibles, percentage deductibles, sub-limits and valuation conditions as well as through rate. An account with a documented story on roof condition, hail mitigation and prior claims is in a different conversation than one without.

Cargo thefts drop in Q2 but losses more than double

Insurance Journal, citing Verisk's CargoNet · Aug 14 · industry data
Cargo theft incidents fell 26% in Q2 2026 against Q2 2025, while the value of goods stolen more than doubled to $304.6m from $135.7m across 677 recorded incidents. Why it matters: the most directly useful data in today's edition for a manufacturer, and the divergence is the story. Fewer thefts taking far more value is consistent with a shift toward targeted, intelligence-led theft — including strategic cargo theft, where loads are obtained through fraudulent pickup and carrier identity misuse rather than taken from a yard — though the figures establish the divergence rather than its cause. Three consequences. First, test transit and stock throughput limits against maximum single-shipment and accumulation values rather than against an average; programme structures vary enough that no general claim about sub-limit adequacy is safe. Second, recovering from the trucker is not the same as being insured: a motor carrier's liability may be limited by released-value provisions, tariffs or contract terms, and how far those limits hold depends on mode, jurisdiction and whether the limitation was properly established. Third, and most important, confirm the wording covers the loss mechanism — fraudulent pickup involves the goods being handed over voluntarily, which runs directly into voluntary-parting, trick-or-device and fraudulent-bill-of-lading exclusions found in many cargo and stock throughput forms. The controls that reduce it are verification controls rather than physical ones: confirming carrier identity, checking driver and tractor against the booking, and avoiding double-brokered loads.

US Climate Prediction Center sees 69% chance of historic El Niño

Insurance Journal, citing the US Climate Prediction Center · Aug 14 · government forecast
The Climate Prediction Center puts the chance at 69% that the developing El Niño exceeds the strength of any event since 1950 during October–December 2026 — a higher bar than the separate figure of greater than 90% for a "very strong" event over autumn and winter. Persistence probabilities are put at 100% through March and 97% through April. Forecasters said conditions may rival 1997. Why it matters: a government forecast rather than vendor research, and the only item today with a multi-quarter planning horizon. Read the percentages carefully — they answer different questions (record strength, versus "very strong", versus how long it lasts), which is why they do not line up with one another. The insurance-relevant point is that El Niño's effects are regional and partly offsetting rather than uniformly bad: the best-established tendencies are suppressed Atlantic hurricane activity through increased wind shear, a wetter southern US winter and a drier northern one, with elevated flood and mudslide risk in California. Those are tendencies across many seasons, not a forecast for any single storm or site, and quiet basin seasons have still produced destructive individual landfalls. The narrow, real planning use is to review winter exposures now at sites in the affected regions — freeze, flood, roof snow load and utility interruption — together with the business interruption consequences of a multi-day outage, including any waiting period and whether service interruption cover extends off-premises. Nothing here justifies scaling back hurricane preparation.

Data center boom fuels rising fire, water and business interruption losses

Risk & Insurance, citing insurer Allianz Commercial · Aug 12 · insurer claims study
Allianz Commercial analysed 221 data centre claims totalling about €677m ($782m). Fire drove over 50% of total insured losses while water damage was the most frequent cause at 21% of claims. It reported 79% of global data centre capacity sits in areas with acute natural hazard exposure, estimated climate risk could reduce the discounted value of the global data centre base by $388bn or 38% of asset value, and projected the data centre insurance market growing from $11bn to over $24bn by 2030. Emerging risks flagged include lithium-ion battery thermal runaway in server racks and multi-storey construction complexity. Why it matters: claims experience from an insurer's own book, which is useful — though shaped by that insurer's underwriting selection, deductibles and client mix, so not a market-wide picture. The transferable finding is the severity-versus-frequency split: fire is the loss that costs, water is the loss that happens. That pattern holds in most industrial property books too and argues for engineering spend on the low-frequency high-severity peril without ignoring the mundane one. The lithium-ion point reaches a manufacturer directly and not only through data centres — battery energy storage, electric forklift charging areas and stored battery inventory all present thermal runaway exposure, and underwriters increasingly ask about it by name. Whether existing protection is adequate is an engineering question rather than a yes-or-no one: it depends on chemistry, state of charge, storage configuration, separation, detection and the applicable NFPA or FM design basis, which is the review worth commissioning rather than assuming either way. Treat the $388bn figure as a modelled estimate of asset value at risk, not an insurance loss projection.

Wildfire risk grows despite lower catastrophe losses

CIR, citing Swiss Re Institute · Aug 12 · reinsurer researchcurrency as printed
CIR reported global insured natural catastrophe losses of an estimated £31.1bn in the first half of 2026, 16% below the 10-year average, alongside a warning that wildfire risk continues to grow. The figure is given here as CIR printed it; Swiss Re Institute normally publishes these estimates in US dollars, so the currency should be confirmed against the primary sigma release before the number is quoted externally. Why it matters: a below-average first half is the backdrop to every soft-market comment in today's edition — the industry is running under budget on catastrophe losses so far this year, which is the context for the broker view in the lead item. Two cautions. A quiet first half says nothing about the second, since the Atlantic hurricane season peak falls in the third quarter and first-half figures have preceded both benign and severe full years. And "below the 10-year average" is not "low" — that average now embeds several very heavy years. The wildfire warning is the part with a manufacturing edge: wildfire underwriting has moved to site-specific scoring on vegetation, defensible space and construction, so a plant in a wildland-urban interface area may find its terms turn on a survey rather than on the account's overall loss record.

Tornado Alley's on the move and creating new risk concerns, modeler says

Insurance Journal, citing catastrophe modeller Verisk · Aug 14 · modeller commentary
Verisk Weather Solutions vice president Tory Farney said Tornado Alley is expanding or shifting eastward into more densely populated regions. National tornado counts are not rising, but losses are worse when tornadoes strike, with average reconstruction values up 72% on 2022 levels. Illinois recorded 220 confirmed tornadoes through July 2026, with Indiana and Wisconsin also setting record highs, driven largely by June and July events. Farney said hail, wind and flash flooding are shifting on the same pattern, and that research remains open on whether climate change or oceanic oscillations are driving it. Why it matters: close to home for an Illinois-headquartered manufacturer, and the record Illinois tornado count through July is the most concrete fact in it. Hold the geographic claim more loosely — an eastward shift in severe convective storm activity is a live research question rather than a settled finding, and Verisk sells catastrophe models. The reconstruction-value figure is the practical half, but it should prompt a test rather than a conclusion: a 72% move in an average can reflect changes in the mix of what was damaged as well as unit cost inflation, and residential reconstruction cost does not transfer cleanly to industrial plant and machinery. The right response is a valuation review at Midwest sites — appraisal-based where values are material — covering both building values against current construction cost and business interruption values against current throughput and realistic machinery lead times.

Jury says Medtronic owes $88 million in first trial over Covidien's hernia mesh

Insurance Journal · Aug 5 · trial verdictstill relevant
After a three-week Boston trial, jurors found Medtronic's Covidien unit failed to give adequate warning of the dangers of a hernia mesh implanted in 2017, awarding $88m. Insurance Journal reported it as by far the largest of more than a dozen hernia mesh and transvaginal mesh cases tried to date. Why it matters: carried from 5 August because today's sweep produced no newer verdict, and it is the most instructive recent result for a manufacturer. The finding was failure to warn rather than defective design, and that distinction is worth understanding precisely: failure-to-warn liability turns on what the manufacturer knew or should have known, what risks were scientifically knowable, and what it told users — so it draws on engineering risk analysis and testing records as well as on labelling and post-market surveillance. It is the version of product liability most likely to reach an industrial manufacturer, because it can attach to a product that performed exactly as designed. Note the posture: this is a first-trial result subject to post-trial motions and appeal, and single verdicts in mass-tort programmes are poor predictors of aggregate outcomes. Two things belong in the coverage conversation alongside it — whether products-completed-operations limits are adequate, and the fact that the cost of recalling or replacing the product itself is generally excluded from general liability by the sistership exclusion and needs separate recall cover.

Product-defect presumption lives on: Florida appeals court upholds $103m against Ford

Claims Journal · Aug 5 · appellate rulingstill relevant
A Florida appeals court upheld a $103m jury award against Ford over a modified Mustang that exploded in a 2017 incident, leaving the owner with severe burns. The coverage focused on the survival of the product-defect presumption. Why it matters: an appellate ruling generally carries further than a trial verdict, though how much further depends on the jurisdiction, whether the opinion was published, which issues were actually decided and whether further review remains available — a Florida appellate decision binds Florida trial courts and is at most persuasive elsewhere. The detail worth carrying into a design and legal review is that the vehicle had been modified after sale, and that did not defeat liability. For any manufacturer whose products are foreseeably altered, serviced or re-purposed by customers, that is the exposure: courts generally ask whether the modification was foreseeable and whether it was a superseding cause of the harm, not simply whether the product conformed to specification when it left the factory. The defensible position is documented foreseeability analysis — what alterations were anticipated, what was warned against, and what the instructions said.

Law restricts her from reducing 'excessive' $91m injured worker award, judge finds

Insurance Journal · Aug 5 · post-trial rulingstill relevant
A judge found she was statutorily restricted from reducing what she described as an "excessive" $91m award to an injured worker. Why it matters: carried for the mechanism, since today's reporting does not set out the underlying facts in enough detail to summarise responsibly beyond the ruling itself. A trial judge concluding an award is excessive but that she lacks the power to reduce it is the clearest illustration of why remittitur rules — and the statutory limits placed on them — matter as much as the verdict number when a company estimates exposure. Note what this is not: a trial judge declining to reduce an award is not an appellate court affirming it, and the award may still be subject to appeal. One distinction is essential before reading this across to an industrial programme. An injured employee's recovery against their own employer is generally confined to workers' compensation benefits under exclusive remedy, so a nine-figure award of this kind ordinarily indicates a third-party action — a worker suing the manufacturer of the equipment involved, or an action-over claim brought back against an employer through a contractual indemnity. A manufacturer's exposure to this pattern is therefore as a third-party defendant on the products side, and as an employer only through employers' liability and action-over routes. Worth confirming what an excess casualty tower would do against a nine-figure single-claimant award, including attachment, whether defence costs sit inside or outside limits, aggregation, and the fact that insurability of punitive damages varies by state.

Samoa adaptation plan calls for regional cat bond, expansion of Pacific parametric coverage

Artemis · Aug 14 · sovereign risk financing
Samoa's national adaptation plan recommends adding drought and flood triggers to Pacific parametric risk transfer products under PCRAFI 2.0, integrating those triggers into the national disaster management plan to allow disbursement within 10 days, and exploring a regional catastrophe bond tranche by 2040 — potentially a multi-country bond issued by a development bank, or reinsurance support for PCRIC or a PCRAFI 2.0 risk pool. Why it matters: sovereign risk financing with no direct corporate application, carried for one design point that does transfer. The plan specifies its deliverable as cash within 10 days, and that is the right thing to nail down in any parametric structure — the number of days from event to payment and what evidence the payment requires. But speed is only one of the criteria: a parametric contract should also be judged on how well the payout correlates with the actual loss, because that correlation is what determines its economic value. Basis risk is not an acceptable side effect to wave through; it is the central design question, alongside trigger transparency, the data source's reliability, geographic granularity, payout caps, price and how the cover coordinates with the indemnity programme underneath it. The 2040 horizon on the cat bond element also indicates these are long-dated policy proposals rather than imminent market activity.

Bermuda Stock Exchange market share grows, with now $70.5bn cat bond and ILS listings

Artemis · Aug 14 · exchange data
The Bermuda Stock Exchange reported a record $70.5bn of ILS-related listings as of 30 June 2026, up from $68.5bn the prior quarter. It listed 146 securities worth about $16.5bn in the first half, of which $16.12bn related to catastrophe bond notes and programmes, and housed 775 cat bond and ILS listings at mid-year, with cat bonds accounting for $62.6bn and the exchange holding 95.4% of the market for 144A and private catastrophe bonds. Why it matters: a venue statistic rather than a risk item, carried because it sizes one of the contributing factors behind persistently soft property catastrophe conditions. Be careful not to overstate it: listings are a measure of what is registered on one exchange rather than of new deployable capital, and catastrophe bonds attach to defined reinsurance and retrocession layers and perils. Primary property market conditions also reflect traditional reinsurance capacity, carrier surplus, loss experience, investment returns and demand. So this is one input into why the broker in today's lead item expects rates to stay soft, not the explanation. For a corporate buyer the effect is indirect — felt through what reinsurance costs its carriers — since cat bonds are not an instrument a buyer of this size issues.

Cyberattacks on outside suppliers threaten automakers with millions in liability

Risk & Insurance · Aug 13 · analysisestimated loss ranges
A March 2026 cyberattack on a breathalyzer technology provider directly affected 15,000 to 30,000 vehicles out of roughly 150,000 users, with per-vehicle losses of $500 to $2,000 depending on duration and usage type. Aggregated availability-driven losses were estimated at $7.5m to $60m, potential class-action exposure at over $100m depending on participation and settlement dynamics, and corporate incident response and remediation costs at tens of millions of dollars. The analysis referenced a 2023 Rivian software update issue and 2024 vulnerabilities in Kia's connected vehicle platform. Why it matters: the most transferable item today for any manufacturer with connected or software-dependent products. The figures are analytical estimates across a wide range, not settled claims, and should be quoted as ranges or not at all. The structure is what to take from it: a supplier's outage disabled a function in vehicles already sold, so the exposure presents as third-party claims against the vehicle manufacturer rather than as its own lost production — and those claims could be framed in warranty, contract, consumer protection or tort, each with different coverage consequences. Two coverage points need stating precisely. Traditional business interruption requires covered physical loss or damage at the insured's own location, and traditional contingent business interruption requires it at a dependent property — so a supplier's systems failing does not trigger either without a specific cyber or system-failure extension. And on the liability side, a standard general liability policy's definition of property damage does include loss of use of tangible property that is not physically injured, so the instinct that GL simply excludes loss of use is wrong; what usually defeats these claims instead is the impaired property exclusion, the "your product" exclusion, the absence of an occurrence, or the claim being purely economic loss. That makes it a wording question rather than a settled answer. The ones worth asking now: is technology errors and omissions or cyber liability in place, does it reach products already in customers' hands, and what do the supply contracts say about indemnity and liability caps when the supplier is the point of failure.

UK factories face rising cyber disruption risk

CIR, citing trade body Make UK · Aug 12 · trade-body survey
Make UK's Cyber Security in Manufacturing Report found nearly 30% of manufacturers experienced a serious cyber incident directly or through their supply chain, with 70% reporting no operationally disruptive incident. Among those affected, 46% saw increased operational costs and production downtime, 31% faced supply chain delays, 31% reported reduced capacity and 23% faced shortages of components or materials. Chief executive Stephen Phipson said "Cyber risk cannot be eliminated altogether. What matters is understanding where the vulnerabilities lie, taking sensible steps to reduce them..." The report identified remaining gaps in patching, incident response planning, senior management oversight and recovery testing. Why it matters: a trade body surveying its own members — a self-selected sample from an organisation with a stake in the policy conversation — and UK data, so directional rather than a base rate for a US manufacturer. What survives those caveats is the distribution of consequences among firms that were hit: the reported effects are operational first, then supply chain. Note that these are counts of firms reporting each consequence, not a measure of which costs most, so it does not establish that operational loss outweighs ransomware, regulatory or liability costs in dollar terms. The four named gaps are the ones worth checking internally, and recovery testing is the one most often assumed rather than verified — a backup regime that has never been restored under time pressure is a plan, not a control. It also counts incidents arriving through the supply chain alongside direct compromises, which is the same channel the automaker item above describes.

NCOIL readopts distracted driving model law as states report double-digit crash drops

Insurance Business America · Aug 13 · model lawnon-binding
NCOIL readopted its Distracted Driving Model Act at its 2026 Summer National Meeting in Boston. The article cites Ohio recording double-digit reductions in distracted-driving crashes and overall fatalities since enactment, Iowa traffic deaths falling 27% against 2024 to a century-low fatality rate in 2025, and Colorado a 19% drop in inattentive-driving crashes within five months of its law, drawing on Ohio State Highway Patrol and Department of Public Safety data combined with telematics, plus research from the Governors Highway Safety Association and Cambridge Mobile Telematics. Why it matters: the status matters more than the statistics. NCOIL is an organisation of state legislators and its model acts bind no one; readoption signals legislative direction, not a change in law, and nothing about any company's obligations changed this week. The reason to track it is that hands-free legislation spreading state by state changes the evidentiary environment for auto liability — where such a law exists, phone use at the time of a crash is a statutory violation and, in many states, evidence of negligence, which strengthens a plaintiff's case against a commercial fleet. Treat the crash-reduction figures as associations reported by advocates of the model rather than established causal effects; several are single-state before-and-after comparisons without controls. The practical response is unchanged and cheap: a written distracted-driving policy covering all business driving, including personal vehicles used for work, is worth having regardless of local statute, because it is the document produced in discovery.

Almost all top US companies had board diversity rules. Now most are gone

Insurance Journal, citing ESGAUGE analysis for Bloomberg News · Aug 14 · governance data
ESGAUGE analysis of corporate filings found 61 S&P 100 companies have eliminated explicit board diversity criteria since 2023, including Apple, Alphabet, Amazon, Starbucks and Wells Fargo. Spencer Stuart's George Anderson said the share of companies with Rooney Rule-like provisions fell to 12% from 58% in the past year. Why it matters: a governance item with a D&O edge, applying to public company boards rather than to manufacturing operations. The exposure is symmetrical, which is why it belongs in a CFO's field of view rather than only a general counsel's: companies have faced securities and derivative claims both for failing to deliver on diversity commitments they made, and for maintaining programmes alleged to breach anti-discrimination law. Removing a commitment reduces one exposure and can create another, particularly where prior public statements to investors are inconsistent with current practice. The defensible position is the same in either direction — a board-level rationale documented at the time the change is made, and disclosure that matches actual practice. Direct application to a private or subsidiary operation is limited, though the same reasoning applies to any public commitment on which performance is later measured.

EEOC says Nike complied with subpoena, drops lawsuit

Insurance Journal · Aug 14 · procedural
The Equal Employment Opportunity Commission has dropped a lawsuit that sought to enforce a subpoena, saying Nike complied with it. Why it matters: a short procedural item, carried for the route rather than the underlying dispute, which today's reporting does not detail. The transferable point is that an EEOC investigation reaching the stage of a subpoena-enforcement action becomes a public court proceeding with its own defence costs, entirely separate from whether any discrimination finding ever follows — and that compliance ended it. Employment practices liability wordings vary considerably in whether and how they fund the defence of administrative proceedings and subpoena enforcement before a charge matures into a claim; some respond only once a formal charge is filed, and some treat agency proceedings as noticeable circumstances rather than claims. That is worth confirming in advance rather than at the point a commission letter arrives.

US home insurance claim severity hit record high

Beinsure, citing LexisNexis Risk Solutions · Aug 13 · personal lines data
LexisNexis Risk Solutions found US home insurance all-peril claim severity reached a record high in 2025, up 25.9% on 2024 and 93.2% against 2019 — the highest across the seven-year study period. Beinsure reported the increase was driven primarily by the January 2025 Los Angeles wildfires, which accounted for $61.2bn in losses. Why it matters: personal lines, carried for context rather than as a benchmark, and it needs reading against its own caveat. Because the 2025 result is dominated by a single catastrophe, it is a statement about catastrophe mix as much as about underlying repair costs — so it illustrates loss severity rather than quantifying construction cost inflation, and it should not be presented to a CFO as measuring the latter. Nor do commercial property values follow it: plant, equipment and process construction have their own cost curves. It belongs in the same conversation as the Verisk reconstruction-value figure above rather than as independent confirmation of it, since both are shaped by the same post-2020 cost environment. The reason to note it at all is that a seven-year doubling in average claim severity, whatever its composition, is the environment in which underwriters are scrutinising declared values.

QBE's adjusted profit climbs to $1.03 billion in half-year result

Insurance Business America · Aug 13 · carrier results
QBE reported adjusted net profit after tax of US$1,033m for the half year to 30 June 2026, up from US$997m, with return on equity of 17.7% against a medium-term target of 15% plus. Group-wide average renewal rate increase was 0.3% for the half, down from 2.1%. Crop insurance ran an 86.0% combined operating ratio with a current accident year result of 94.1%, improved from 95.2%. North America saw temporary volume reductions from underwriting actions in accident and health and workers' compensation programme exits. Why it matters: a carrier result, and the number a buyer should notice is the 0.3% group renewal rate increase, down from 2.1%. It is worth weight because it comes from a party with the opposite commercial interest to a broker — an insurer has no incentive to understate the rate it is achieving — and it points the same direction as today's broker commentary. But it is a group-wide average across many territories, currencies and lines, and cannot be read as pricing for US industrial property or casualty specifically. Note too that strong profitability may reflect prior-year rate earning through, reserve development, light catastrophe experience and investment income rather than current pricing. The reasonable inference is limited but useful: healthy carrier returns at roughly flat rate are the conditions in which competition tends to intensify, which supports expecting buyer-friendly terms to persist into the next renewal cycle rather than tighten.

Westfield Specialty grows premium 25% in H1 2026

Insurance Business America · Aug 13 · carrier results
Westfield Specialty grew premium 25% in the first half of 2026, with second-quarter premium up 20% and the combined ratio improving two points. Why it matters: one carrier's growth figure, carried as a small data point on market direction. Premium growth of that size in a roughly flat-rate environment is more likely to reflect volume, new business and mix than price — though the reported figures alone do not establish that, since growth can also come from exposure changes, acquisitions or timing, and an improving combined ratio does not indicate appetite for any particular risk. The usable implication is about market access rather than pricing: carriers in expansion mode are generally the ones most willing to quote new business and to compete for an account, which is an argument for marketing a programme more broadly this year rather than defaulting to incumbents. Reinsurers rethink growth as pricing discipline tightens

Beinsure · Aug 5 · reinsurer executive commentarystill relevant
Active Re chief executive Ramón Martínez Carrera said "capacity remains available, competition has intensified, and pricing terms are under pressure" and that the company had "maintained a selective approach", prioritising portfolio quality and technical profitability. He said a growing number of established reinsurers and MGAs are prioritising sustainable profitability over volume growth. Active Re reported an 88.4% combined ratio, 16.1% return on equity and a $26.7m technical result. Why it matters: executive commentary from one reinsurer rather than market data, included as a third voice describing conditions consistent with today's broker and carrier items — a broker, a large global insurer and a reinsurer all reporting capacity ahead of demand. Treat the two halves differently: the observation about pricing pressure is corroborated elsewhere today, while the claim that the market is exercising discipline deserves more scepticism, since every underwriter describes their own book as disciplined and it is the standard thing to say in a softening market. The direction is what a buyer can use, and it is consistent — supply is ample. The corollary worth planning around is that soft markets typically end faster than they begin, usually on a capital event rather than gradually, which is the argument for using this window to fix the structural things that are cheap to fix now: limits and sub-limits, valuation accuracy, and multi-year terms where a carrier will write them.
