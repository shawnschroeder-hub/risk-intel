No. 014 — MONDAY, AUGUST 17, 2026 — 15 ITEMS — 12-SOURCE ROSTER
CFO Brief — three takeaways, written to be read aloud
  1. Exposure — the Midwest flood over the weekend is a reminder that flood sits outside ordinary property cover, and that the gap is easy to underestimate. The White River crested at nearly 25 feet at Anderson and Noblesville, Indiana, more than a foot above the 1913 record, killing at least seven people and knocking out power to roughly 130,000 customers at its Sunday evening level, down from a peak near 300,000. The number that should travel to a CFO is not the crest but the coverage gap behind it: NFIP caps residential building cover at $250,000 and contents at $100,000, and climate risk modeller First Street has found roughly twice as many US properties sit in flood-prone areas as FEMA's maps show — after Hurricane Helene in 2024, about 98% of damaged homes in western North Carolina were outside FEMA floodplains. Those are residential figures and First Street is a commercial modelling firm with its own methodology, so neither transfers directly to an industrial site — and property damaged outside a FEMA Special Flood Hazard Area is not by itself proof the map was wrong, since the SFHA has never been a representation that everywhere else is safe. The transferable point survives all of that: flood is not covered as an ordinary peril in most commercial property programmes but written subject to a separate sub-limit, deductible and often a separate DIC or standalone placement, and in some programmes those terms are tiered by FEMA flood zone. The question worth asking this week is what the flood sub-limit and deductible are at each Midwest plant, what the annual aggregate is, whether the tiering rests on a FEMA zone determination or on modelling and elevation survey, and how the policy defines a high-hazard flood zone.
  2. Regulatory — the US has formally objected to the EU sustainability rules that reach US manufacturers through their EU business, not through their headquarters. The administration sent a letter to the EU, with the US Ambassador stating publicly that the US "will take any actions necessary to address unreasonable burdens on US commerce" if the Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive and the Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive are not modified. Be precise about status, because it is the whole story: this is a diplomatic objection and a threat of unspecified measures, not a rule change, a tariff or a suspension of anything. Both directives remain in force, and both can reach a non-EU parent through its EU activity rather than through where it is headquartered — but only above defined thresholds and on phased application dates, with the due diligence directive transposed through member-state law, so "does EU business" is not the test and the applicability analysis is a real piece of work. The EU is still drafting compliance guidance for the due diligence directive and the Commission had not responded when the piece was filed. The exposure a CFO should understand is that the due diligence directive reaches into the supply chain, and directors' and officers' as well as general liability wordings were not written with a mandatory environmental and human rights due diligence regime in mind. The planning line is to keep compliance work on the existing timetable — a threat of retaliation is not a safe harbour — while asking the broker what a CSDDD-driven claim would actually be, and which policy would be expected to answer it.
  3. Market — there is a specific, checkable question to put to the broker about what cyber cover is quietly losing at renewal. Cyber insurer Resilience reported wrongful data collection claim frequency of 45.9 per 100 policies in the first half of 2026, up from 40.5, while only 3.4% of those matters produced an incurred loss — the lowest across five reporting periods — with individual settlements typically $10,000 to $30,000. Read the frequency carefully: with fewer than four in a hundred producing any incurred loss, the count is evidently of notifications and demands rather than of paid claims, so it is not comparable to a claims frequency in the sense a CFO would assume. Their global head of claims described it as "high volume, low severity" and said that "because this is becoming so common, we're also seeing more and more exclusions." That last point is one carrier describing its own book and, indirectly, its own appetite — it is not evidence that the cyber market as a whole is narrowing, and separate broker-authored guidance this month on AI exclusions names no insurers or forms, so it corroborates the concern rather than the trend. What both justify is a review, not a conclusion: read what came off the policy at the last renewal, not just what the premium did. Pixel, tracking and session-replay technology on customer-facing web properties is the ordinary route into a wrongful data collection claim, and it is usually a marketing decision rather than an IT one — which is why the exposure tends to sit outside the risk function's line of sight.

01

Record Indiana flooding kills seven as flood insurance gaps come into focus

Insurance Business America, citing climate risk modeller First Street · August 17 · Catastrophe

At least seven people died as the White River crested at nearly 25 feet at Anderson and Noblesville, more than a foot above the 1913 record. More than 350 people were evacuated in Delaware County alone, with hundreds more along the White River corridor, and nearly 130,000 customers were still without power by Sunday evening, down from a peak near 300,000. A federal emergency declaration followed. The article sets FEMA's 2026 individual assistance cap at roughly $43,900 combined for housing and other needs, and NFIP limits at $250,000 for residential building and $100,000 for contents, and cites First Street research finding twice as many US properties in flood-prone areas as FEMA mapping shows — noting that after Hurricane Helene in 2024, roughly 98% of damaged homes in western North Carolina sat outside FEMA floodplains.

Why it matters — the closest event of the weekend to a Midwest manufacturing footprint, and the reason to lead with it is the gap rather than the crest. Read the figures for what they are: the assistance and NFIP numbers are residential and have no application to a commercial site, and First Street is a commercial modelling firm whose flood model differs from FEMA's by design, so "twice as many" is one vendor's estimate rather than an agreed correction — and damage outside a Special Flood Hazard Area does not prove the mapping is wrong, since the SFHA marks a regulatory threshold rather than the limit of flood risk. What survives all of that is the structural point. Flood is rarely an ordinary covered peril in a commercial property programme: it is carved out and written back subject to its own sub-limit, deductible and annual aggregate, sometimes within the all-risk form and sometimes through a DIC or standalone placement, and in some programmes those terms are tiered by FEMA flood zone. Where that tiering applies, the adequacy of the cover rests on a map that at least one commercial model, and now a second major inland flood in two years, suggests is not the full picture. Worth checking at Midwest sites while this is live: the flood sub-limit, deductible and annual aggregate and whether the limit is shared with other catastrophe perils; whether the tiering rests on FEMA zone, a catastrophe model or an elevation survey, and how the policy defines a high-hazard zone; and whether dependent-property cover would respond where a key supplier or a utility substation sits in a floodplain even though the plant does not — noting that most contingent wordings require damage by a covered peril at a scheduled location, so an unscheduled supplier or a flood-excluded dependent property may leave nothing to claim. Inland riverine flooding of this kind often builds over days, which can leave time for physical mitigation in a way a flash flood does not — but this same event produced flash flooding and seven deaths, and no property protection measure is worth keeping people on site for.

02

Wrongful data collection exclusions are spreading — what brokers need to check in cyber policies

Insurance Business America, citing cyber insurer Resilience · August 17 · Carrier claims data

Resilience reported wrongful data collection claim frequency of 45.9 claims per 100 policies in the first half of 2026, up from 40.5 in the previous period, while only 3.4% of claims generated an incurred loss — the lowest across five reporting periods — with individual settlements typically running $10,000 to $30,000. Jeremy Gittler, the insurer's global head of claims, described it as "more of a volume situation… high volume, low severity" and said that "because this is becoming so common, we're also seeing more and more exclusions." He said smaller businesses are increasingly exposed because larger corporations tend to have legal departments familiar with privacy requirements, and that underwriters are asking more about data collection practices, retention periods, customer notification and third-party vendor oversight. California was named as prominent because of its privacy statutes.

Why it matters — a carrier describing claims on its own book, so the frequency is a fact about Resilience's portfolio and its underwriting selection rather than a market base rate, and an insurer noting that exclusions are spreading is also, indirectly, describing its own appetite. Note what the 45.9 figure counts. With only 3.4% producing an incurred loss, the great majority of these matters are notifications, demands or claims closed without payment, so the number describes reporting activity rather than paid claim frequency and should not be quoted as the latter. The mechanism is what transfers. Wrongful data collection claims generally arise from tracking technology on customer-facing web properties — advertising pixels, analytics tags and session-replay tools — brought under wiretap, privacy or video privacy statutes. Why they resolve rather than go to trial is a separate question the article does not answer, and defence economics, statutory damages exposure and class certification risk are at least as likely an explanation as the underlying technology. Two consequences for a manufacturer. First, the exposure is created by marketing and web teams rather than by IT or the plant, so it sits outside the usual cyber risk conversation and will not be found by a controls assessment aimed at ransomware. Second, and more important given the trend the article reports: if this cover is being narrowed or excluded at renewal, the change may not be visible in the premium at all. That makes it a wording comparison exercise — this year's form against last year's — rather than a pricing one. Worth asking the broker for a marked-up comparison at the next renewal, and worth asking marketing what tracking runs on the corporate and e-commerce sites and under what consent regime.

03

US ready to 'take any actions necessary' to fight EU ESG rules

Insurance Journal · August 17 · Diplomatic action · No rule change

The administration sent a formal letter to the EU objecting to the Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive, which requires reporting on ESG-related financial risk, and the Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive, which requires companies to identify, disclose and remediate environmental and social impacts. The US Ambassador posted that the US "will take any actions necessary to address unreasonable burdens on US commerce" if the rules are not modified, arguing they disadvantage American companies. Both apply on the basis of business done in the EU rather than on where a company is headquartered. The EU is drafting compliance guidelines for the due diligence directive; the European Commission had not responded at the time of publication.

Why it matters — the status is the story, and it is easy to overstate in either direction. Nothing has changed in law: this is a diplomatic objection plus a threat of unspecified measures, the directives remain in force, and no compliance obligation has been suspended, delayed or narrowed by anything reported here. Companies that scale back EU compliance work on the strength of a letter would be taking a real risk on a political outcome. Two qualifications on scope, because the summary above compresses them. "Applies on the basis of EU business" does not mean every company with an EU customer: both directives turn on turnover and size thresholds and phase in over several years, and the due diligence directive takes effect through each member state's implementing law, so applicability is a specific analysis rather than a general condition. And the due diligence obligation is better described as identifying and addressing adverse human rights and environmental impacts in a company's own operations and chain of activities, with remediation arising in defined circumstances, rather than a flat duty to remediate everything. The insurance-relevant edge is that directive rather than the reporting one, because its obligations extend beyond the company's own gates and contemplate liability — a liability-shaped regime rather than a disclosure-shaped one. Where that lands in an insurance programme is genuinely unsettled: it is not obviously general liability, which responds to bodily injury and property damage; it is not obviously directors' and officers', though claims alleging misstatement of compliance or failure of oversight could be; and many wordings have regulatory and fines-and-penalties provisions whose reach varies by jurisdiction and by whether the penalty is insurable at all. That is a question to put to the broker now, while it is hypothetical.

04

Illinois gives insurance regulator power over home and auto insurance rates

Beinsure · August 12 · Enacted legislation · Still relevant

Governor J.B. Pritzker signed House Bill 4273 and Senate Bill 714, effective 1 July 2027, giving the Illinois Department of Insurance authority to review homeowners and auto rate filings and to determine within 60 days of filing whether rates are excessive, inadequate or unfairly discriminatory. The homeowners bill requires 60 days' notice before premium increases above 10% and mandates use of state-specific claims data where available; the auto bill requires 30 days' notice above the same threshold and addresses non-driving rating factors such as credit scores and ZIP codes. Both allow the department to order rebates. Industry groups opposed the measures, arguing they risk reducing availability and raising long-term costs.

Why it matters — carried from Wednesday because it is enacted law in an important manufacturing state, and because the reflex reading of it is wrong in both directions. It is personal lines only — homeowners and private passenger auto — so it does not touch commercial property, casualty, workers' compensation or a corporate auto fleet, and nothing in a commercial programme changes because of it. Note also the date: the effective date is 1 July 2027, so even for personal lines this is a 2027 event being reported in 2026. The reason to track it at all is directional, and the label matters: what is described is a review-and-disapproval power exercisable within 60 days of filing, which is not the same as a prior-approval regime in which rates cannot be used until approved, and the reporting does not establish which filing mechanism applies. What it does establish is that a department in the country's fifth-largest state has been given intervention powers it did not have, and the department that reviews personal lines rates is the same one that handles commercial form and rate filings, market conduct examinations and surplus lines administration. That is a reason to watch rather than a basis for predicting anything about commercial regulation. The industry's stated concern — that constrained pricing reduces availability — is the standard argument made against rate regulation and should be read as a position rather than a finding, though it is the mechanism regulators are watching for. For an Illinois employer there is also a small, real personal-lines angle: this is what employees' own home and auto renewals will look like from mid-2027.

05

SEC to keep hands off shareholder proposals, worrying activists

Insurance Journal · August 17 · Regulatory policy

The SEC made permanent its decision to stop issuing "no-action" letters on shareholder proposals — the letters that previously told a company the staff would not object if it excluded a resolution from its proxy. The freeze was instituted in November and is now policy. The Commission said the change lets its Division of Corporation Finance concentrate on broader filing reviews, citing an extensive existing body of guidance on exclusions. A law firm count showed 66% of known proposals appeared on proxies as of mid-June, up from 59% a year earlier, and of six lawsuits brought over exclusions, five produced outcomes favourable to proposal supporters. Chair Paul Atkins criticised chief executives as "lackadaisical" about using the new policy.

Why it matters — a public-company governance item with a direct D&O consequence, and the consequence is procedural rather than ideological. The no-action process was a cheap administrative route for resolving whether a proposal could be excluded; removing it does not change the substantive rule under Rule 14a-8, it changes who decides and at what cost. A company that wants to exclude a resolution now has three options rather than one: put it to a vote, omit it on its own legal analysis and accept the risk of a shareholder suit or Commission action, or seek a declaratory judgment. Only the last converts what was a staff letter into litigation with its own defence costs and public record, and it is a choice rather than a requirement. The five-of-six figure is a very small sample and should not be read as a success rate, but it does indicate that companies taking the litigation route have not been finding it easy. Two things follow for a CFO. Expect more proposals to reach proxies, which raises the volume of shareholder engagement work regardless of how the votes land. And be clear about the D&O position, which is worse than "check the wording": a declaratory action the company itself brings is not the defence of a claim made against an insured, so it generally falls outside the standard D&O insuring agreement altogether unless specific wording or a counterclaim changes the analysis. Budget it as an uninsured legal cost unless the broker confirms otherwise. Direct application to a private or subsidiary operation is limited.

06

IDB arranges its first parametric catastrophe swap for Belize, Swiss Re backs it

Artemis · August 17 · Sovereign risk transfer

The Inter-American Development Bank arranged a $20m parametric hurricane risk transfer for Belize running from 1 August 2026 to 31 May 2028, covering the 2026 and 2027 hurricane seasons, with payouts capped at $10m per policy year and triggered on pre-agreed parametric conditions. Swiss Re provided the entire coverage. Belize contributed toward the premium with support from the Ready and Resilient Americas Initiative and donor contributions from the French Climate Fund. The arrangement sits alongside $25m of contingent financing and $118m of approved IDB loans carrying climate resilient debt clauses that allow payment postponement after qualifying disasters. It is the IDB's first executed parametric catastrophe swap.

Why it matters — sovereign risk financing with no direct corporate application, carried for two design features that do transfer to a corporate parametric purchase. The first is the per-year cap: $20m of cover with a $10m annual limit across a two-season term is a deliberate choice to spread capacity rather than concentrate it, and the equivalent question for a corporate buyer is what happens after the first event — whether the cover reinstates, whether the annual aggregate is exhausted, and what the programme looks like for the rest of the season. Buyers routinely check the trigger and skip this. The second is the multi-instrument structure: the parametric swap sits alongside contingent debt and postponement clauses, each doing a different job. Parametric cover is genuine risk transfer, but non-indemnity risk transfer — it pays on a measured parameter rather than on proven loss, which is what makes it fast and what makes the payout imperfectly correlated with the damage. Basis risk is therefore the central design question rather than a footnote. That does not confine it to a supporting role: parametric structures are legitimately used to fund a large deductible, to substitute for a layer that the indemnity market prices badly, or to cover economic loss that indemnity cover will not reach at all, such as revenue lost with no physical damage. What it should not be is a quiet swap for indemnity cover on the same exposure without the basis risk being measured and accepted deliberately.

07

Beazley partnered with Integral ILS on new cyber ILS fund platform in Bermuda

Artemis · August 17 · Capital markets · Launch targeted late 2026

Beazley has partnered with Integral ILS Ltd., which manages over $3.5bn of third-party investor capital as of 2026, to establish a cyber insurance-linked securities fund platform in Bermuda, with a holding company, reinsurer and fund structure registered to support it. Launch is targeted for late 2026. Beazley chief executive Adrian Cox said the partnership "brings together our market leading cyber underwriting expertise" with Integral's capital-raising capability. Artemis reported it as Integral's first move into specialty reinsurance beyond natural catastrophe risk, and noted it could become the first dedicated multi-investor cyber ILS fund strategy at scale rather than a bespoke arrangement.

Why it matters — a structural item rather than a news event, and it should be read as an intention with a target date rather than as capacity that exists today. The reason it belongs in a buyer's brief is the constraint it addresses. Cyber is the line where capacity is most sensitive to the fear of correlated, systemic loss — one vulnerability or one cloud provider affecting thousands of insureds at once. That accumulation concern is one of several reasons cyber limits have stayed modest relative to demand and systemic-event and war-adjacent exclusions have stayed tight; model uncertainty, legal uncertainty over what the exclusions actually mean, reinsurance terms and plain carrier appetite all contribute, and it would be too neat to credit any one of them. Third-party capital entering the line, if the vehicle raises and deploys, is one of the few developments that could ease the accumulation constraint specifically. Two cautions. Investors have historically been wary of cyber precisely because it lacks the long event history and independent measurement that make catastrophe risk modellable, so the fund raising is not a given. And capital arriving at the reinsurance and retrocession level does not automatically reach the primary buyer as either broader wording or bigger limits: carriers can expand capacity while holding systemic exclusions firm, and for most buyers the binding issue is the exclusion rather than the limit. Worth watching into the 1 January renewal season as an indicator of where cyber capacity is heading, not as something that changes this year's placement.

Mangrove secures record catastrophe reinsurance cover for 2026 hurricane season

Artemis · August 17 · Carrier reinsurance placement · No economics disclosed

Mangrove Property Insurance Company, a Florida property and casualty specialist, placed what Artemis described as a record-setting catastrophe reinsurance programme for the 2026 Atlantic hurricane season, extending to a 1-in-175 year event and including aggregate protection for third and fourth occurrences. The most concrete component is a debut $111m Buttonwood Re Ltd. (Series 2026-1) catastrophe bond providing capital markets protection against Florida named storm losses. Artemis reported the programme drew on over 30 reinsurers and that Mangrove recently established Grove Re Ltd., a Bermuda sidecar, to widen access to third-party capital. No total programme limit was disclosed.

Why it matters — a Florida carrier's own reinsurance purchase, included as a possible supply signal rather than for its Florida specifics, and "record" is Artemis's characterisation of the programme rather than a market statistic — no price, attachment point, total limit or prior-year comparison is disclosed, so there is nothing to verify it against. Read it for the terms rather than the adjective, and hold even those loosely. A programme reaching a 1-in-175 year return period with aggregate cover for third and fourth events, supplemented by a first-time catastrophe bond, is the kind of structure that is easier to assemble when capacity is plentiful — though a count of more than 30 participating reinsurers is as consistent with a placement spread thinly across many small lines as with abundance, and without the economics there is no telling which. It is directionally consistent with the soft conditions this brief has been tracking for two weeks rather than evidence of them. The read-across for a corporate buyer is weaker still and belongs in the straw-in-the-wind category: reinsurance cost sits upstream of what primary carriers can offer, but this is one Florida catastrophe programme, Florida property reinsurance has dynamics of its own, and nothing in it measures what industrial property elsewhere will renew at.

09

Tyson to close more beef plants

Insurance Journal · August 14 · Corporate restructuring

Tyson is closing an Illinois beef plant and a Utah case-ready facility and pursuing a sale of a Washington beef plant, after shutting a Nebraska facility earlier. The Illinois and Washington plants process roughly 5,000 cattle daily; group slaughter capacity has fallen by about 10,000 head since early 2025 to an estimated 18,000–20,000 per day. The company said the changes aim "to create a more competitive footprint amidst one of the most historic cattle shortages the country has ever experienced," and plans to concentrate beef operations around three plants in Nebraska, Kansas and Texas, returning the Amarillo facility to two shifts as cattle become available. JBS and Cargill have also reduced capacity.

Why it matters — not an insurance story on its face, carried because plant rationalisation is the operational event that most reliably generates insurance and risk work at a manufacturer, and because it is a live example of an input shortage forcing a footprint decision. The risk consequences run in several directions at once and are frequently missed in the transition. An idle or partially idle plant is a different property risk from a running one — sprinkler and fire protection systems still need to be maintained and monitored, and watchman and impairment procedures apply. Property wordings handle unoccupied premises in materially different ways: some restrict specified perils such as vandalism or water damage after a set period, some require notice or a vacancy permit, some impose safeguard conditions, and manuscript programmes often negotiate the point out entirely. What is consistent is that the carrier needs telling before the plant goes quiet rather than after, and that the applicable condition needs reading rather than assuming. Workers' compensation prospective exposure does fall with payroll, but the claims experience of a closure does not track the headcount curve — separations, reduced-duty transfers and end-of-employment medical reporting tend to surface claims, including cumulative-trauma allegations, on the way out. Employment practices exposure rises with any large-scale reduction, and WARN Act notice obligations sit alongside it. On the sale of the Washington plant, the liability for products already made and shipped from that site does not transfer with the real estate — but neither is the answer automatically a run-off purchase. What responds depends on when the injury occurred against the occurrence-based policies in force at the time, on whether any relevant cover is claims-made, and on how the sale agreement allocates retained liabilities and indemnities. That is a transaction question to settle in the documents rather than a coverage question to discover later. The broader signal — three major meatpackers cutting capacity on the same input constraint — is the more general lesson for any manufacturer whose raw material supply has tightened structurally rather than cyclically.

Protecting your organization from AI insurance exclusions

Risk Management Magazine (RIMS), by a broker executive · August 4 · Practitioner guidance · Older but critical

Writing in RIMS' magazine, Founder Shield managing director Kyle Jeziorski describes five artificial intelligence exclusions appearing in commercial renewals: claims from inaccurate AI recommendations excluded as "unvetted machine-learning decisions"; automated content creation barred from media liability; employees entering corporate data into public AI tools treated as gross negligence; physical harm traced to generative system advice excluded from general liability; and algorithmic errors affecting physical infrastructure blocked by "machine learning process" exclusions. He recommends auditing renewals for terms such as "algorithmic processes" or "autonomous decision-making", establishing AI governance with human-in-the-loop review, and negotiating affirmative AI endorsements or standalone products.

Why it matters — carried at nearly two weeks old because it is the practical companion to today's lead cyber item, both describing cover narrowing quietly at renewal, and because nothing newer this weekend covers the same ground. Weigh the source properly: this is a broker executive writing in a trade association's magazine, and brokers have a commercial interest in clients seeking endorsements and specialist placements. The article does not name insurers or forms, and describes patterns rather than a surveyed market, so treat the five categories as a checklist of what to look for rather than as evidence of how widespread each has become. That said, the checklist is genuinely useful, and the fifth category is the one that reaches a manufacturer hardest: exclusions written around "machine learning processes" affecting physical infrastructure can, depending on wording, reach automated quality inspection, predictive maintenance, robotic handling and process control — systems that are ordinary factory equipment now and are not thought of as AI internally. The practical step is unglamorous and cheap: ask the broker to identify every AI, algorithmic or autonomous-systems term across the property, casualty, cyber and technology E&O wordings, then map those terms against what the plants actually run. The exposure created by a broadly drafted exclusion is not the AI project anyone is arguing about — it is the machine that has been on the line for three years.

Guilty plea entered as insurers continue paying the security cost of an executive's killing

Insurance Business America, citing an analysis of corporate filings · August 17 · Governance / executive protection

Following the guilty plea in the December 2024 killing of UnitedHealthcare's chief executive, Insurance Business reported that elevated executive security spending across the sector has proved durable rather than temporary. UnitedHealth Group disclosed $1.7m of executive security spending for the year of the shooting, roughly four times the corporate average, with the largest single allocation nearly $927,000 for protection of one subsidiary chief executive. More than a third of the largest US-listed insurance companies increased executive security spending afterwards, on an analysis of corporate filings.

Why it matters — a sombre item, carried for the durable governance point rather than the case. The finding worth noting is that spending has held up a year and a half later, which indicates boards reclassified executive protection as a standing cost rather than an incident response — and the disclosure route matters, since executive security paid by a company is generally reportable as a perquisite in proxy disclosure unless it qualifies as a business expense under specific conditions, which is why these figures are visible at all. For a manufacturer the transferable questions are narrower than the headline suggests, but they are real: whether kidnap, ransom and extortion cover is in place and whether it extends to threat response, security consultancy and relocation costs as well as ransom; whether workplace violence cover exists, which is a distinct product responding to on-premises incidents including counselling, business interruption and site remediation; and whether any crisis management or crisis response provision in the D&O programme funds anything useful in the first 48 hours. General liability is not part of that conversation — it responds to third-party bodily injury and property damage claims, not to protecting or responding to an attack on the company's own executives — and any of these covers may require a credible threat, may be limited by territory, and may require the use of an insurer-approved response consultant. Publicly identifiable executives at companies in contested public debates carry the elevated exposure, and the ordinary controls — travel protocols, home security assessments, restraint about publishing executive schedules and locations — cost far less than the cover.

12

DIFC Court of Appeal limits reinsurer defence-cost liability

Beinsure · August 12 · Appellate ruling · Still relevant

In Al Buhaira National Insurance Company v Arab War Risks Insurance Syndicate, a $70m marine war risk reinsurance dispute arising from the tanker M/T Beta, which disappeared and later surfaced in Iran as MV Makran, the DIFC Court of Appeal overturned a lower court decision that would have required the reinsurer to cover potentially unlimited defence costs, while confirming English law governs the contract. The panel of Chief Justice Wayne Martin and Justices Sir Peter Gross and Patrick Keane held that any implied duty to reimburse defence costs clashed with the written limits in the reinsurance contract, and rejected an argued regional market practice of automatic reimbursement of legal expenses unless expressly excluded. The cedant won a secondary point: the reinsurance terms document formed part of the agreement despite never being signed by the reinsurer.

Why it matters — a reinsurance dispute between two insurers with no direct corporate application, carried for the habit of mind it illustrates rather than for any precedential force. Take the limits seriously: this is a reinsurance contract governed by English law, decided in a Gulf financial-centre court on its own wording, and it decides nothing about a US direct insurance dispute, where governing law, the policy language and the course of dealing between the parties would control. What travels is the disposition. The court held that an implied duty to reimburse defence costs could not stand against the written limits in the contract, and declined to give effect to an asserted regional practice of automatic reimbursement — which is not a ruling that a properly proven trade custom can never bear on interpretation, but is a reminder that a practice everyone believes in is a weak foundation when the wording says otherwise. That is worth internalising before the next renewal, because a good deal of what companies believe about their own programmes rests on how things have always been handled: that defence costs sit outside the limit, that a long-standing broker understanding will be honoured, that a market practice will fill a gap. Defence costs are where this bites hardest, since whether they erode the limit or sit in addition to it is among the largest economic terms in any liability policy and is frequently assumed rather than read. The secondary holding cuts the other way — an unsigned terms document was still held to form part of the agreement — which is a reminder that documents can be incorporated into a contract by the parties' objective conduct even where the signing formalities were never completed, so placement documentation should be treated as capable of binding rather than as draft until executed.

13

EBRD to expand Ukraine war-risk reinsurance facility after full use

Beinsure · August 12 · Development finance · Still relevant

The EBRD and Aon are expanding their Ukraine war-risk reinsurance facility after strong demand, with the EU identified as a primary supporter. The Ukraine Recovery and Reconstruction Guarantee Facility reached full capacity in roughly nine months from its March 2025 launch, insuring €360m of cumulative goods and vehicle movements against an original guarantee size of €110m. Separately, the European Commission approved a €1.5bn reinsurance scheme for war-related transport risks in Ukraine, under which Poland's export credit agency KUKE assumes 80% of the war risk with insurers retaining 20%. The EBRD deployed €550m in Ukraine through its trade facilitation programme in 2025, with private companies accounting for 57% of its Ukraine investment volume.

Why it matters — narrow in direct application, carried because it illustrates how war risk cover is actually being manufactured in the current environment, and that is worth understanding by any company with cargo, contractors or subsidiaries near a conflict. The structure is the point: in both schemes a public balance sheet stands behind risk that private markets would not carry alone — explicitly so in the Commission-approved transport scheme, where the Polish export credit agency takes 80% and insurers retain 20%, and through a development bank guarantee in the EBRD facility, whose risk split the reporting does not give. Read that as evidence of where private appetite runs out, not as a claim that no war risk cover exists without a state behind it. Note also what "reached full capacity" means: the facility's guarantee capacity was fully committed within about nine months, not exhausted by paid losses, and the €360m of insured movements against a €110m guarantee reflects shipments turning over rather than losses. The practical reads for a manufacturer are three. Capacity of this kind is finite and is allocated first-come, so cover for goods moving in or near a conflict zone is a planning exercise weeks ahead of the shipment rather than a purchase at the point of need. Terms are shaped by whichever public scheme applies, which means the answer changes by route and by jurisdiction. And it is worth being clear about the starting position in the existing programme: war on land is not a peril the standard commercial property market buys back by endorsement — it is largely uninsurable there, and cover comes, if at all, from specialist standalone political violence and war markets or from government pools. Marine and cargo are the exception, where war risk cover is a long-established separate placement.

Insurance Business America · August 17 · Catastrophe

Hurricane Lala, a Category 1 storm, skirted the Big Island without making landfall but delivered up to 32 inches of rain, triggering flash floods and mudslides that displaced more than 100 properties, causing one storm-related death and leaving more than 200,000 households without power. Insurance Business set it against Hawaii's unusual market structure, where hurricane wind, flood and standard property cover are separate products with distinct carriers, triggers, deductibles and claims processes — homeowners policies exclude both hurricane wind and flood, with flood losses falling to the NFIP or private flood policies. The fragmentation dates to Hurricane Iniki in 1992, a Category 4 storm causing about $3bn of damage that prompted insurer withdrawals and the creation of the Hawaii Hurricane Relief Fund. The 2023 Maui wildfires, which destroyed over 2,200 structures and generated $3.2bn of insured losses, had already strained capacity.

Why it matters — the second flood item today, and the pairing is the reason to run it. A storm that never made landfall and never reached major hurricane strength still produced significant loss, and the damage the reporting describes — flash flooding and mudslides after up to 32 inches of rain — is water damage rather than wind damage, though nothing in the reporting rules out a wind component in the outages and other losses. That is the same pattern as the Indiana event above in a completely different setting, and the lesson is the recurring one: catastrophe planning that indexes on wind speed and landfall category underweights the peril that often causes the damage inland and uphill. The structural half is a useful illustration of coverage segmentation taken to its extreme. When wind, flood and all-risk cover sit with different carriers on different triggers, the exposure moves into the seams between them — which peril caused which damage, how concurrent causation is treated, and which deductible applies. Mudslide is the sharpest example: depending on wording it can fall under flood, under earth movement, or outside both, and anti-concurrent-causation language can decide the outcome before the facts are argued. Industrial programmes are usually more integrated than Hawaii's residential market, but the same seams exist wherever flood, earth movement or named windstorm carry their own sub-limits and percentage deductibles, and they are worth walking through with the broker before a claim rather than during one.

Insurance Journal · August 17 · Cyber incident

Hackers accessed Liechtenstein's confidential registry of foundations and trusts, covering roughly 31,000 entities, obtaining beneficial owners' names, birth dates, nationality and place of residence. Why it matters — a government breach in a small jurisdiction, carried for the pattern rather than the target. Two features are worth extracting. First, the stolen data is identity and ownership information rather than payment data, which is the more damaging category over time — beneficial ownership details cannot be reissued the way a card number can, and their value to an attacker is in enabling later fraud, impersonation and social engineering rather than in immediate monetisation. Second, the ransom position was stated before any demand arrived, and that sequencing is the transferable discipline: deciding the policy in advance, at board level, is what prevents the decision being made under duress by whoever happens to be in the room at 2am. Where a cyber policy is in place, the decision remains the company's to make, but the reimbursement does not: most extortion covers require the insurer's prior consent to a payment, so paying without it can mean paying alone. Sanctions exposure is the harder constraint — a payment to a designated person or entity can be unlawful regardless of what any policy says, which makes screening the recipient an essential step before payment rather than a formality. Worth confirming in advance who is authorised to decide, what the policy requires by way of consent and notice, and that the incident response retainer is with a firm the insurer has already approved.
